Propulsion Advances
High-power electric and plasma systems are advancing toward practical interplanetary and
efficient terrestrial use. NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory tested a lithium-fed
magnetoplasmadynamic (MPD) thruster in February 2026, reaching 120 kW—more than 25
times the power of the highest-power electric thrusters on any current NASA spacecraft (such
as those on Psyche). The thruster uses high currents interacting with a magnetic field to
accelerate lithium plasma electromagnetically. The team aims for 500 kW to 1 MW per thruster,
supporting nuclear-electric propulsion architectures that could shorten Mars transit times. The
central electrode exceeded 2,800 °C; materials endurance under prolonged high-temperature
operation remains a core challenge.

NASA Fires Up Powerful Lithium-Fed Thruster for Trips to Mars - NASA

Parallel work includes rotating detonation engines (RDEs) for hypersonic applications. GE
Aerospace demonstrated scalable RDC (rotating detonation combustion) in ramjets with a
threefold airflow increase versus prior demonstrators. [omitted: out of scope] These detonation-based

systems offer higher thermodynamic efficiency than conventional combustion but remain
developmental for sustained flight.

On the terrestrial and atmospheric side, MagLev Aero’s HyperDrive uses magnetic-levitation
rim-drive technology for ducted fans. Permanent magnets and electromagnetic systems
suspend and drive the rotor at its perimeter (eliminating a central shaft), reducing tip-vortex
losses. Demonstrations (including a tethered hover of a heavy drone with four Tempest units)
and DARPA funding target higher thrust per power, lower noise, and VTOL applications. Claims
of substantial efficiency gains (tens of percent more thrust for the same power in some
configurations) come from reduced tip leakage and optimized multi-blade designs; full flight
data and long-duration reliability are still emerging.

Maritime progress is pragmatic and nearer-term: battery-electric ferries, hydrogen fuel-cell

voltage DC power systems for zero- or low-emission vessels. These emphasize efficiency,
modularity, and reduced emissions rather than novel physics.

Honest limitations: Plasma and MPD systems excel in specific impulse but produce low thrust

drives still face thermal, control, and certification hurdles. None yet approach the continuous,
high-lift, low-power profiles we explore in POL eddy-current models over conductive surfaces.

Energy Harvesting Advances
Ambient and distributed harvesting is moving from laboratory curiosities toward practical low-
power applications. A University of Arkansas / Michigan graphene inductor harvests energy

from the magnetic fields of ordinary 60 Hz power lines to run nanowatt-scale temperature
sensors, storing charge in capacitors for multi-hour autonomy even without continuous input.

UMass Amherst researchers created a biohybrid mesh harvester: an ultrathin, porous polymer
scaffold seeded with human cardiac cells that grow around distributed ribbons of lead zirconate
titanate (PZT). The cells’ contractions mechanically deform the piezoelectric elements,
generating electricity. Laboratory results showed more than 10× the effective power density (by
volume) of conventional centralized harvesters. The architecture is deliberately distributed—
mirroring biological networks rather than a single generator. It remains lab-scale;
biocompatibility, long-term integration, and scaling to usable power levels are open questions.

Thermophotovoltaic systems converting extreme heat (thermal batteries) into electricity at

Honest limitations: Most ambient harvesters produce microwatts to low milliwatts—excellent

bandwidth matching, and materials durability under real environmental conditions continue to

Tesla Roadster: Can it modulate suction, and is there feedback data?
Yes, the new Roadster is designed to modulate suction.

Tesla’s multi-mode fan + ground-sealing system (unveiled October 2026) has two primary
operating modes:

Low-speed “sucker” mode: Fans pull air from under a sealed underbody (deployable skirts)
to generate downforce even at 0 mph. Tesla/Musk claims up to ~2 g (demo showed ~4,700
kg / ~2 g while inverted on a ramp). This is useful where passive wings do almost nothing
(roughly 0–50 mph).
High-speed mode: Front and rear skirt sections open; the fans then help drive a high-flow
venturi under the car while the active wings take over. Wings themselves are actively
pitched (Optimus-derived actuators, sub-0.25 s adjustments claimed) and switch between
high-downforce, low-drag/DRS, and road modes.

Control inputs described in patents and stage commentary include speed, acceleration,
steering, brake, GPS/map data, and underbody sensors (radar, ultrasonic, cameras/lasers
for ground clearance and skirt position). The system is intended to make continuous
micro-adjustments.

Feedback data status (honest limits):

There is on-stage telemetry from the reveal (downforce readouts, g-forces during the inverted
ramp and thruster demos), manufacturer claims, and patent descriptions of closed-loop
sensing and actuation. Independent, high-resolution public datasets (pressure maps, power-
vs-downforce curves, real-road leakage/feedback loops, durability under debris, etc.) are not
yet available. This is still prototype/reveal-stage information. Real-world sealing on imperfect
roads, energy cost (~80 kW peak for the four fans in the high-power claims), and long-term
feedback stability remain open engineering questions.

The suction is an active pressure-difference system (fans + seals), not a passive ground-effect
diffuser alone — conceptually closer to historical fan cars (Chaparral 2J, Brabham BT46B,
McMurtry Spéirling) than to conventional aero.

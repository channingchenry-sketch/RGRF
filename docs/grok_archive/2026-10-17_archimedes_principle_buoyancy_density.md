The Stomachion (Ostomachion / Loculus of Archimedes)
This is a 14-piece dissection puzzle that tiles a square. The pieces sit on a 12 × 12 grid and have
relative areas 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 4, 4, 4, 7, and 8 (absolute areas multiples of 3 that sum to 144).
Two pairs of pieces are congruent.

Later classical sources describe it as a game in which the pieces could be rearranged into figures —
animals, ships, towers, and so on — much like a tangram. The surviving fragmentary text in the
Archimedes Palimpsest, however, strongly suggests Archimedes was interested in a deeper
question: how many distinct ways can the fourteen pieces be assembled back into the square?

Modern enumeration (Cutler, 2003; independently confirmed by combinatorial methods of Chung,
Diaconis, Holmes & Graham) gives:

    17,152 solutions if rotations and reflections are counted as distinct,
    536 geometrically distinct solutions once rotations, reflections, and swaps of identical pieces
    are treated as the same.

This is one of the earliest documented combinatorial investigations. Archimedes appears to have
been counting the possible rearrangements of a fixed set of tiles into the same overall shape —
precisely the kind of enumeration that later became a branch of mathematics in its own right.

Mitochondria in Neurons
Neurons have exceptionally high energy demands. Synaptic transmission, ion pumping (especially
Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase), axonal transport, and calcium buffering all require continuous ATP. Mitochondria
supply most of that ATP through oxidative phosphorylation and also serve as local calcium buffers.

In axons and at synapses, mitochondria are not static. They undergo regulated fission, fusion,
transport (anterograde and retrograde), and mitophagy. Their positioning is activity-dependent:
synapses that fire frequently tend to recruit and retain more mitochondria. When mitochondrial
function or trafficking fails, the most distal and energy-hungry regions (synaptic terminals and long
axons) are the first to suffer.

Neuropathy
Many peripheral neuropathies have a strong mitochondrial component. Common patterns include:

    Energy failure in long axons → length-dependent axonal degeneration (the classic “dying-back”
    pattern).

    Impaired axonal transport of mitochondria, leading to distal energy starvation.

    Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) that damage axonal membranes, proteins, and DNA.

    Defects in mitochondrial dynamics (too much fission or failed fusion) that fragment the network
    and reduce efficiency.

    Calcium dysregulation when mitochondria can no longer buffer synaptic or nodal calcium loads.

This appears in diabetic neuropathy, certain toxic and chemotherapeutic neuropathies, and a
number of genetic neuropathies (including some forms of Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease that
directly affect mitochondrial proteins or dynamics). The result is often a combination of sensory,
motor, and autonomic fiber loss, with pain, numbness, weakness, and impaired reflexes.

Synaptic Responses
At the synapse, mitochondria play three interlocking roles:

1. ATP supply for vesicle recycling, active zone maintenance, and postsynaptic signaling
   cascades.

2. Calcium buffering that shapes the time course of residual calcium and therefore short-term
    plasticity (facilitation, depression).

3. Local redox and metabolic signaling that influences longer-term plasticity and synaptic
   homeostasis.

When mitochondrial function declines, several synaptic phenotypes appear:

    Reduced quantal content or impaired vesicle replenishment (weaker transmission).

    Altered short-term plasticity because calcium clearance is slowed.

    Increased threshold for long-term potentiation or, conversely, lower threshold for long-term
    depression in some circuits.

    Greater susceptibility to excitotoxicity when calcium buffering fails.

In chronic neuropathy these synaptic changes compound the axonal problems, further degrading
reliable signaling.

Regenerative and Systems Notes
Mitochondria are plastic. Exercise, certain metabolic interventions, and signals that promote
mitochondrial biogenesis or improve quality control (mitophagy) can partially restore function in
experimental models. Because neurons cannot easily divide, supporting the existing mitochondrial
population and its trafficking is one of the more promising levers for slowing or partially reversing
neuropathic decline.

The same bioenergetic and calcium-handling principles that make synapses vulnerable also make
them potential sites for targeted support — local ATP delivery, improved calcium buffering, or
modulation of mitochondrial dynamics.

    Subventricular zone (SVZ) lining the lateral ventricles — newborn neurons migrate long
    distances along the rostral migratory stream to the olfactory bulb.

    Subgranular zone (SGZ) of the hippocampal dentate gyrus — newborn granule cells integrate
    locally into existing circuits.

Outside these niches, constitutive neurogenesis is minimal in most adult mammals, although limited
reactive neurogenesis can occur after injury in some regions. Developmentally, of course,
neurogenesis is widespread and tightly patterned along spatial axes (dorsal–ventral, anterior–
posterior, and local zonal gradients of morphogens).

Key regulatory features include:

    Niche signals (Notch, Wnt, Sonic hedgehog, BMP, neurotransmitters, and local vasculature).

    Activity-dependent modulation — enriched experience, exercise, and certain forms of learning
    increase hippocampal neurogenesis; chronic stress and aging reduce it.

    Mitochondrial status of the progenitors themselves — shifts in oxidative metabolism and
    mitochondrial dynamics influence the decision between continued proliferation and neuronal
    differentiation.

Zonal Transport Mechanisms
“Zonal” here can refer to two related but distinct systems:

1. Transport within and from neurogenic zones (neuronal migration)

Newborn neurons must leave the niche and travel to their final positions. This involves:

    Tangential and radial migration guided by chemotactic cues, cell–cell adhesion, and extracellular
    matrix.

    In the SVZ–rostral migratory stream system, chains of neuroblasts move inside glial tubes; the
    migration is saltatory and depends on both actin–myosin contractility and microtubule-based
    transport of organelles.

    Leading-process extension and somal translocation require local ATP and calcium signaling, so
    mitochondrial positioning inside the migrating cell is critical.

2. Axonal (and dendritic) transport — zonal organization along the process

Once a neuron has extended an axon, long-range transport becomes essential. Cargoes
(mitochondria, synaptic vesicle precursors, RNA granules, endosomes, autophagosomes) move on
microtubules via kinesin (anterograde) and dynein (retrograde) motors.

Zonal features appear in several ways:

    Proximal vs distal axon — different cargo sets and motor regulation; distal axons are especially
    vulnerable when transport slows (a major factor in length-dependent neuropathies).

    Nodes of Ranvier and synaptic terminals — local zones of high energy demand that actively
    capture and retain mitochondria.

    Injury-response zones — after axonal damage, a local “zone” of calcium elevation and
    cytoskeletal reorganization determines whether the axon regenerates or degenerates;
    retrograde signaling (including transport of injury-induced cargoes) informs the cell body.

    Mitochondrial transport zones — stationary mitochondrial pools at synapses and nodes versus
    motile pools that redistribute according to activity. Fission/fusion balance and motor–adaptor
    regulation (e.g., Miro/Milton, syntaphilin) create functional zones of high or low mitochondrial
    density.

In neuropathy, defects in these zonal transport systems — especially the failure to deliver healthy
mitochondria to distal axons and synapses — amplify energy failure and synaptic dysfunction.
Conversely, interventions that improve mitochondrial trafficking or support axonal transport can
slow distal degeneration and, in some models, aid regenerative attempts.

Linking the Two
Neurogenesis supplies new neurons; zonal transport mechanisms determine whether those neurons
(or existing ones) can maintain long processes and functional synapses. Mitochondrial health sits at
the intersection: progenitors need appropriate metabolic shifts to differentiate, migrating neurons
need local energy for movement, and mature axons need continuous zonal delivery of mitochondria
to survive and transmit.

Yes — let’s examine basal metabolic rate (BMR).

Definition and Measurement
Basal metabolic rate is the minimum energy expenditure required to maintain essential physiological
functions while at complete rest, in a neutrally temperate environment, and in the post-absorptive
state (typically after a 12-hour fast). It covers breathing, circulation, cellular maintenance, ion
pumping, and basic organ function.

It is most accurately measured by indirect calorimetry (oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide
production under strict resting conditions). In practice the term “resting energy expenditure” (REE)
is often used for measurements made under slightly less stringent conditions; the two are close but
not identical. Predictive equations (Harris–Benedict, Mifflin–St Jeor, and others) estimate BMR from
age, sex, height, and weight, but measured values are preferred when precision matters.

BMR typically accounts for 60–75 % of total daily energy expenditure in sedentary adults.

Major Determinants
    Body size and composition — Lean mass (especially muscle and highly active organs) is the
    strongest driver. Larger bodies and higher fat-free mass raise BMR.

    Age — BMR declines roughly 1–2 % per decade after early adulthood, largely from loss of lean
    mass and changes in organ metabolic rate.

    Sex — On average higher in males, mainly because of greater average lean mass and differences
    in body composition.

    Hormones — Thyroid hormones are primary regulators; other endocrine factors (growth
    hormone, sex steroids, catecholamines) also modulate it.

    Genetics and individual variation — Substantial inter-individual differences remain after
    accounting for the above.

    Clinical state — Fever, inflammation, certain diseases, and medications can raise or lower BMR.

At the cellular level BMR reflects the sum of ATP turnover across tissues — ion pumping, protein
synthesis and turnover, mitochondrial proton leak, and maintenance of membrane potentials.

Brain and Nervous System Contribution
The adult human brain is only ~2 % of body mass yet consumes approximately 20 % of resting
metabolic rate (and a similar fraction of oxygen). In childhood the proportion is even higher, peaking
around 65 % of resting metabolism in early childhood when the brain is both large relative to the
body and highly active in synaptogenesis and remodeling.

Most of that energy supports neuronal signaling (action potentials and synaptic transmission) rather
than simple cellular maintenance. This high, relatively constant demand explains why mitochondrial
function, axonal transport, and local energy supply at synapses are so critical — and why energy
failure rapidly produces neurological consequences, including the distal vulnerabilities seen in
many neuropathies.

Relation to Earlier Threads
BMR is the whole-body expression of the same bioenergetic principles we examined at the cellular
and organelle level. Mitochondrial efficiency, density, and dynamics in muscle, liver, and especially
neural tissue directly influence measured BMR. In neuropathy, local energy failure in axons and
synapses can occur even when whole-body BMR appears normal, because the deficit is regional and
length-dependent. Supporting mitochondrial quality and transport can therefore improve local
energy status without necessarily producing large changes in whole-body BMR.

Yes — cholesterol is a natural next step.

Core Roles
Cholesterol is a sterol lipid essential for animal cell membranes. It modulates membrane fluidity
and permeability, packs between phospholipids, and helps form specialized microdomains (lipid
rafts) that organize signaling proteins. It is also the precursor for steroid hormones, bile acids, and
vitamin D.

In the nervous system its importance is amplified:

    Myelin is extremely rich in cholesterol; oligodendrocytes and Schwann cells require large
    amounts for proper myelination and maintenance of the myelin sheath.

    Synaptic membranes depend on cholesterol for vesicle fusion, receptor clustering, and
    postsynaptic density organization.

    Mitochondrial membranes also contain cholesterol, which influences membrane protein
    function and certain transport processes, although the content is lower than in plasma
    membranes.

Because the blood–brain barrier largely prevents circulating cholesterol from entering the CNS,
nearly all brain cholesterol is synthesized locally, primarily by astrocytes and, to a lesser extent, by
neurons and oligodendrocytes. Turnover in the adult brain is slow; cholesterol is conserved and
recycled with high efficiency.

Synthesis, Transport, and Regulation
Cholesterol is made via the mevalonate pathway, with HMG-CoA reductase as the rate-limiting
enzyme (the target of statins). In the periphery it is transported in lipoproteins (LDL, HDL, etc.).
In the brain the main transporters and carriers are different — APOE-containing lipoproteins,
and specialized molecules such as ABCA1 and ABCG1 mediate cholesterol efflux from cells to
these carriers.

Cellular cholesterol levels are tightly sensed by SREBP and LXR pathways that adjust synthesis,
uptake, and efflux. Excess free cholesterol is esterified and stored, or exported.

Links to Neuropathy and Synaptic Function
    Disorders of cholesterol or related lipid metabolism can produce peripheral neuropathies,
    sometimes through impaired myelination or altered membrane properties of axons and Schwann
    cells.

    At synapses, acute or chronic changes in membrane cholesterol alter neurotransmitter receptor
    function, ion-channel behavior, and the efficiency of exo- and endocytosis. Both too little and
    excess cholesterol can disrupt plasticity.

    Mitochondrial cholesterol content, while modest, can affect organelle membrane properties and
    has been implicated in certain neurodegenerative and metabolic stress conditions.

Because neurons and glia have high membrane surface area and continuous remodeling needs,
local cholesterol availability and trafficking become part of the same zonal energy and transport
economy we discussed earlier. Distal axons and synaptic terminals are again vulnerable if supply or
recycling falters.

Systemic Context
Peripheral blood cholesterol levels (LDL, HDL, total) are clinically important for cardiovascular risk
and are influenced by diet, genetics, hormones, and drugs. They do not, however, directly reflect
brain cholesterol status. Interventions that lower circulating cholesterol (statins, diet) have complex
and still-debated effects on the nervous system; some studies note rare neuropathic side-effects,
while others explore possible neuroprotective actions via reduced inflammation or improved
vascular function.

Cholesterol therefore sits at the intersection of membrane integrity, myelination, synaptic signaling,
and cellular energetics — all themes already present in the mitochondrial, neuropathic, and

transport discussions.

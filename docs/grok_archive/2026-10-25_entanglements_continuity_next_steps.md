3. Electricity as T → 0
Absolute zero is a limit, not a bench. Third law: you do not sit on it. What actually happens on
the way down depends on the stuff.

Ordinary metal. Phonon scattering dies (Bloch–Grüneisen). Resistivity falls toward a residual
set by impurities, defects, grain boundaries — not toward zero, unless the sample is a
superconductor. A perfect infinite lattice with no impurities is a thought-experiment, not a wire.

Superconductor. DC resistivity is zero below Tc , which is finite. Persistent current is a
​

condensate, not “thermal motion stopped.” Zero-point motion of the lattice and of the EM field
remains. This is the closest laboratory object to “electricity without dissipation,” and it does not
require T = 0.

Semiconductor / insulator. Carriers freeze out. Conductivity collapses. Millikelvin freeze-out is
a real device problem, not a plasma miracle.

Plasma. Ionization is thermal or photo. As T drops, a classical plasma recombines and dies.
What survives in the lab is an ultracold neutral plasma: laser-cooled atoms photoionized,
electron/ion temperatures from millikelvin to a few kelvin — two orders below old plasma
textbooks, still not zero. Ions can be strongly coupled (Γ = e2 /(akT ) ≳ 1); electrons usually
are not, because disorder-induced heating dumps correlation energy into heat the instant you
ionize. Conductivity then tracks Γ, not a march to perfect conduction. Three-body
recombination rapidly grows Rydberg atoms.

Vacuum. The photon field still has 12 ℏω per mode at T = 0. That is not a current you can wire.
​

Casimir / ZPF engineering lives here as informed speculation unless we specify an apparatus.

So “electricity at absolute zero” splits:

Dissipationless current → superconductivity at finite Tc .           ​

Vanishing current → freeze-out in semiconductors and recombination in plasmas.

Remaining fluctuations → quantum, not a classical pinch.

A Kerr magnetosphere is at millions of kelvin, not millikelvin. You cannot import ultracold-
plasma Γ into BZ and call it the same electricity.

the lab devices first, then what that geometry can and cannot do near a hole.

It would look like a potential well, not a magnetic bottle and not a geodesic bundle. The
equation is Poisson, not Raychaudhuri and not Bennett.

A charge sits where the electrostatic potential Φ has a minimum for that sign of q . Same-sign
charges make the well as they pile up (space charge). Opposite-sign charges try to cancel it.
That last sentence is why electrostatic confinement of a quasineutral fusion plasma is hard, and
why single-sign traps work.

What you would actually see
Four lab geometries, increasing in how much magnetic field they cheat with.

1. Gridded IEC / fusor.

Concentric transparent spheres. Inner grid at negative kV pulls ions inward. Ions overshoot,
form a virtual cathode — a cloud of negative space charge at the center — which then pulls
more ions. In “star mode” the discharge self-organizes into bright spokes through the grid
holes. Neutrons can appear. The grid also intercepts ions, heats, emits electrons, and melts if
you push it. That is the picture people mean by “electrostatic confinement” in fusion posters.

2. Virtual cathode without a live grid (Polywell-class / electron-injected well).

Inject electrons into a cavity until their space charge is a negative well deep enough to hold
ions. The electrons themselves still need something to stay put — often magnetic cusps. So this
is electrostatic for the ions, magnetic for the electrons. Hybrid, not pure E.

3. Penning / Malmberg–Penning.

Axial B for radial confinement, electrostatic end-plugs for axial confinement. Holds a non-
neutral plasma (all electrons, or all ions) for seconds. Looks like a glowing or invisible column
between two biased rings, sitting in a solenoid. Angular momentum conservation plus a single
sign of charge is why it lasts. A virtual cathode can form at the injector if you push current
too hard.

4. Paul / RF quadrupole.

No static well in 3-D is allowed by Earnshaw: ∇2 Φ = 0 in empty space has no interior minimum.
RF micromotion time-averages to an effective well. This is how ion traps and some atomic
clocks look — a few ions, laser-cooled, not a reactor.

Earnshaw is the geometric fact under all of this: static vacuum electrodes cannot trap a charge
in an empty interior point. You need either (a) charge density inside the volume (virtual
electrode), (b) a magnetic field, (c) time-varying fields, or (d) a wall.

For many particles the well is the particles. Poisson says the well depth tracks density. Then

Thermal tail. Once ions Maxwellianize, the tail above the rim leaves faster than they fuse.
Rider’s critique of IEC: you cannot keep a deeply non-Maxwellian core at reactor density;

Grid intercept. Any physical anode/cathode in the beam eats particles and radiates X-rays.

Opposite sign. Electrons and ions together neutralize ρ. The well collapses unless you

Typical stored densities in Penning-class electrostatic-ish traps sit around 1016 –1017 m−3 .
Magnetic bottles of similar size hold more. Neither number is a tokamak core.

Commercial use that does work: IEC as a compact neutron source, not as a power plant. That is

Absolute zero does not invent a new electrostatic bottle. It freezes the thermal leak. Impurities,

No collisions, no B , no metric. The well is whatever ρ and the electrodes write. What comes out
of that system, in every geometry people actually simulate, is not a sealed bottle. It is a
population split: some phase-space volume stays trapped for a while, the rest streams,
reflects, or leaves over the rim.

What “leaky” looks like in the runs
Virtual cathode. Inject current along an axis or toward a sphere. Space charge piles up until Φ
on axis goes past the beam energy. The core reflects. You see a potential minimum that
oscillates, a hollow or stagnated density, and a transmitted halo. That is the MEP / Malmberg-
injection picture and the Child–Langmuir limit with time on it. Observable: oscillating well depth,
reflected current, a dark or hollow core.

Two-stream and phase-space holes. Counter-streaming injected and reflected particles.
Vlasov loves this: trapping islands (BGK-like holes) form in (z, vz ). Those islands are local
​

electrostatic confinement of a subset. They are also leaky — particles phase-mix out, holes
merge or damp. Observable: holes in velocity-space diagnostics, RF at the bounce frequency.

Maxwellian tail, even before collisions. A deep well with a filled core still has particles with
E > qΦrim . In Vlasov they never scatter into the tail if you start them cold and quiet — which is
​

why idealized sheets look better than the lab. Any perturbation, any injection noise, any
electrode ripple populates the tail. Those particles leave on a bounce time, not a collision time.
Observable: evaporative loss, current to the walls, X-rays from the grid.

Quasineutral core. Put both signs in at fusion-relevant density and Vlasov–Poisson erases the
well. Debye shielding is the leak as a principle: λD short means Φ cannot reach across the
​

cloud. That is Rider’s point in kinetic language. IEC “confinement” that survives in simulation
is either non-neutral, or low-density, or only a minority population on radial orbits through a
virtual cathode.

Expansion. Ultracold neutral plasma in Vlasov–Poisson (or hybrid) just flies apart on the ion-
acoustic / self-similar expansion time. No rim. Observable: R(t) ∝ t, cooling of the remaining
well as fast particles leave first.

So yes: the observable state of electrostatic “confinement” is a leaky, often oscillating well
plus a halo. Star-mode spokes, grid glow, virtual-cathode flicker, trap loss rates, UNP expansion
— those are the lab counterparts of what the solver draws.

When the leak slows (still not zero)
Vlasov–Poisson can look almost confined if you stack symmetries:

Single sign of charge (non-neutral).

Axisymmetry and a good B along z (now it is Vlasov–Poisson–Maxwell, i.e. Penning).

Exact conservation of canonical angular momentum.

No collisions.

Then transport is slow and the column lasts seconds. The leak is field errors, end-plug ripple,
residual neutrals — things the ideal solver omits. Add a Dougherty / Fokker–Planck collision
operator and the tail fills on the scattering time; evaporative loss returns even in a pretty well.

What this does not rewrite
Kerr magnetospheres are not Vlasov–Poisson boxes. They are GRMHD / GRPIC on a metric,
with B dominant. A spark-gap virtual cathode there is a local electrostatic anecdote inside a
magnetic engine.

Raychaudhuri still tracks the geodesic subset (photons, neutrals). The leaking charges are the
accelerated subset. Different populations, different equations.

A Dougherty packing can illustrate nested wells and halos. It does not compute the Vlasov tail.

So your sentence is right, with one clause: Vlasov–Poisson gives leaky confinement as the
generic, observable electrostatic state; the exceptions are single-sign, high-symmetry traps
that leak slowly until the world is imperfect — which it is.

If we run one, the useful first shot is 1-D Vlasov–Poisson on an injected beam against a plug:
well depth vs current, bounce-frequency spectrum, fraction over the rim. That is the
breadcrumb that matches a probe trace, not a reactor claim.

Ultrasonic Standing Waves in Water
An ultrasonic standing wave forms when two counter-propagating pressure waves of the same
frequency interfere inside a liquid-filled cavity (or between a transducer and a reflector). The result
is a fixed pattern of pressure nodes (minimum acoustic pressure, maximum particle velocity) and
antinodes (maximum pressure, minimum velocity), spaced λ/2 apart.

Water responds in several measurable ways:

    Acoustic radiation force and primary Bjerknes force move denser or less compressible
    particles/bubbles toward nodes or antinodes depending on their contrast factor.
    Acoustic streaming and local viscous dissipation appear.

    At higher intensities, transient cavitation can occur (with the usual risks of radical formation and
    local heating).

Higher harmonics change the geometry of that pattern. When a transducer is driven not with a pure
sine but with a waveform that includes the fundamental plus its odd harmonics (approximating a
square wave), the nodal planes become thinner and more sharply defined. Pressure gradients
steepen. This is documented in multi-frequency ultrasonic standing-wave systems used for particle
focusing and levitation: adding the 3rd and 5th (or first four odd) harmonics produces noticeably
more stable, tightly localized layers of particles or cells than the fundamental alone.

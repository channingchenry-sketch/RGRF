The four foundational observers

Pituitary–melanopsin relationship
Modern work confirms and extends Hollwich. Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells
(ipRGCs) express melanopsin and project via the retinohypothalamic tract to the
suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). From the SCN the signal reaches the pineal and, through multi-
synaptic routes, modulates the hypothalamic–pituitary axes (including ACTH/cortisol and,
indirectly, α-MSH pathways). Melanopsin is optimized for irradiance detection rather than
spatial detail; it tracks the overall light environment and sets circadian and seasonal endocrine
tone. In some non-mammalian species there is even direct photoreception in pituitary
cells themselves. The energetic pathway Hollwich described is now anatomically and
molecularly grounded.

The fovea
The fovea is the high-acuity specialization: densely packed cones, displaced inner retinal layers
so light reaches the photoreceptors with minimal scatter, and private-line midget pathways that
preserve single-cone resolution. Cajal’s drawings already captured its layered organization. It is
the region of highest spatial and temporal bioelectric fidelity for image-forming vision, while the
more peripheral and melanopsin-rich systems handle the non-image-forming, irradiance-driven
signals that reach the pituitary axis. The fovea is the precision transducer; the broader retina
plus ipRGCs supply the energetic and circadian context.

Benzene rings (aromatic systems)
Aromatic rings — benzene and its derivatives — appear in the side chains of phenylalanine,
tyrosine, and tryptophan. These residues absorb UV light through π → π* transitions and
participate in electron transfer and resonance stabilization within proteins. Many biological
chromophores and cofactors rely on extended aromatic or conjugated systems for light
absorption and charge delocalization. The delocalized π-electrons provide a natural substrate
for resonant electronic behavior and for coupling light energy into conformational or redox
changes. This is grounded photophysics, not speculation: aromatic amino acids are the primary
UV chromophores in proteins, and their photochemistry is well mapped.

Phosphate and light–protein chain interactions
In vertebrate phototransduction the cascade is phosphate-centric. Photon absorption
isomerizes 11-cis-retinal → all-trans, activating rhodopsin (or cone opsins). Activated rhodopsin
is phosphorylated by rhodopsin kinase (GRK1) on C-terminal serines/threonines; multiple
phosphates allow arrestin binding and shut-off. Downstream, the G-protein transducin
activates PDE, which hydrolyzes cGMP (itself derived from GTP). Recovery involves guanylate
cyclase restoring cGMP and dephosphorylation. ATP and GTP phosphate bonds are the energy
and signaling currency; light-triggered conformational change is rapidly converted into a
cascade of phosphate addition/removal. Melanopsin signaling appears to use related G-protein
and phosphoinositide pathways. Light → protein conformational change → phosphate chemistry
is the core molecular chain.

Experimental observations: mitochondria as vibrational transducers

structured acoustic inputs increase ATP synthesis while reducing reactive oxygen species;
other spectra produce neutral or less favorable shifts. Mechanisms under discussion include

Theoretical frameworks for phonon involvement
Several independent lines of modeling propose that quantized vibrational modes (phonons) or
coherent collective excitations participate in mitochondrial energy and charge transport.

Electron-phonon coupling and infrared transmission.

An early quantitative model (Cope, 1973) treated phosphorylation as involving phonons in the
mitochondrial solid state. The phonon was conceptualized as acting like a trapped photon that
could generate reverse photocurrents inhibiting oxidation unless dissipated through
phosphorylation. Infrared optical phonons were proposed to propagate efficiently along the
lipid bilayer geometry, which was modeled as an infrared coaxial transmission line or resonant
cavity. The same framework was extended qualitatively to nerve membrane potentials.

Membrane solitons and proton–energy co-transport.

More recent continuum models describe long-range proton transport along the inner
mitochondrial membrane (cristae) as two-component solitons. One component is a proton kink
propagating along ordered hydrogen-bond chains of interfacial water; the second is an acoustic
compression soliton in the lipid headgroup (cardiolipin-rich) lattice. The coupled excitation
carries both charge and elastic energy with low dissipation toward rows of ATP synthase
dimers. Characteristic frequencies of the headgroup oscillations fall in the 10–100 GHz range,
and soliton velocities on the order of 50–100 m/s have been estimated. This provides a
concrete phonon-like (acoustic soliton) channel linking respiratory-chain proton pumping to
ATP synthesis.

Fröhlich’s nonequilibrium condensation hypothesis remains relevant. Metabolically driven polar
modes can, above a threshold energy input, channel energy into the lowest-frequency
collective vibration, producing macroscopic coherence. Mitochondria supply both the
continuous energy (ATP, proton-motive force) and strong local electric fields that order

Fröhlich condensation — more precise picture
Fröhlich’s 1968–1970s proposal describes a nonequilibrium phase transition in a set of coupled
polar oscillators (membrane segments, protein polar modes, microtubule subunits, etc.). When
continuous energy is supplied faster than it can be thermally dissipated into a heat bath,
nonlinear interactions among the modes cause the energy to condense preferentially into the
lowest-frequency collective mode. The result is a macroscopic coherent oscillation that
behaves like a giant oscillating dipole.

Key features from the models and recent work:

The process is classical in character at physiological temperatures for the relevant
frequencies (sub-THz range, typically 0.1–1 THz), although it originates in a quantum
Hamiltonian.

Threshold behavior: below a critical pumping rate the energy is distributed more or less
equipartitionally; above threshold the lowest mode becomes macroscopically occupied.

Experimental anchors include:
0.4 THz irradiation of lysozyme crystals producing non-thermal, long-lived (µs–ms)
electron-density changes and subtle longitudinal compression of an α-helix — lifetimes
far longer than ordinary vibrational damping would allow, interpreted as condensation.

In our language, Fröhlich condensation supplies a mechanism by which metabolic or optical
energy can be organized into a coherent vibrational state rather than simply thermalized.

Davydov solitons — more precise picture
Alexander Davydov proposed that the free energy released by ATP hydrolysis can be stored and
transported along protein α-helices as a self-trapped quasiparticle. The amide-I vibration
(primarily C=O stretch, ~1650 cm⁻¹ / ~0.205 eV) couples nonlinearly to the acoustic phonons of
the hydrogen-bonded peptide lattice. The localized vibrational energy distorts the lattice; the
resulting lattice deformation reacts back to trap the vibrational energy, preventing dispersive
spreading. The combined exciton–phonon object propagates as a soliton.

The Davydov Hamiltonian contains three terms:

exciton (amide-I) Hamiltonian,

phonon (lattice deformation) Hamiltonian,

nonlinear interaction term proportional to the change in amide-I energy with hydrogen-
bond length.

Simulations and analytical work show that solitons can be launched from the ends of a helix or,
under phase-modulated excitation, from interior sites. Stability at 300 K is parameter-sensitive
and still debated; some numerical studies find that thermal fluctuations destroy self-trapping,
while others indicate that certain initial conditions and coupling strengths allow solitons to
persist for biologically relevant distances. Model systems such as crystalline acetanilide have
provided spectroscopic signatures consistent with self-trapping.

Davydov solitons therefore offer a concrete phonon-mediated channel for non-dispersive
energy transport inside proteins — a microscopic counterpart to the larger-scale soliton
descriptions we saw for mitochondrial cristae membranes.

Biogenic silica in diatoms
Diatoms (unicellular algae) construct species-specific silica cell walls called frustules. These
are hierarchical, nanoporous architectures formed inside specialized intracellular compartments
— the silica deposition vesicles (SDVs). Formation occurs at physiological temperature and
near-neutral pH, in stark contrast to the high-temperature or extreme-pH routes required for
most synthetic silica.

Key molecular players

Silaffins: small, heavily post-translationally modified peptides (lysine residues carrying
long-chain polyamines; serine/threonine residues phosphorylated). They accelerate silicic-
acid polycondensation and influence particle morphology.

Long-chain polyamines (LCPAs): oligo-N-methyl-propylamine chains (up to ~20 repeat
units). They are major organic constituents of the biosilica and can induce rapid silica
precipitation even in the absence of proteins. Species-specific sets of LCPAs help
determine pattern morphology.

Silacidins: acidic phosphoproteins that promote silica formation in the presence of LCPAs.
Cingulins: proteins that assemble into organic microrings; these act as pre-formed
templates that become mineralized into nanopatterned silica girdle-band replicas.

dAnk proteins (ankyrin-repeat proteins): recent work shows they help control pore-pattern
morphogenesis, consistent with liquid–liquid phase separation of organic components
inside the SDV that templates the hierarchical pore arrays (areolae → cribrum → cribellum).

Silica morphogenesis is therefore a protein- and polyamine-guided process involving controlled
phase separation, membrane curvature of the SDV, cytoskeletal positioning, and sequential
deposition. The resulting frustule is not a simple crystal but a hierarchical composite of
amorphous hydrated silica structured by organic templates.

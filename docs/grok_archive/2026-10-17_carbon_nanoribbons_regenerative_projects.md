What Are Carbon Nanoribbons?
Graphene nanoribbons are narrow strips of graphene (a single layer of carbon atoms in a hexagonal
lattice), typically with widths from a few nanometers to tens of nanometers and lengths that can
extend much farther. Unlike 2D graphene sheets, their quasi-1D structure opens a tunable bandgap,
leading to semiconductor-like behavior while retaining exceptional strength, conductivity, and
thermal properties.
Key differences from CNTs (which we already reference in our CNT coils and BOM):
CNTs are rolled graphene tubes (cylindrical).
GNRs are "unzipped" or flat strips—offering higher surface area for composites, better edge
effects for reactivity/tunability, and potentially superior load transfer in matrices.

Synthesis Methods
Top-down: Unzipping multi-walled CNTs (e.g., via chemical oxidation or plasma)—practical and
scalable from existing CNT production.
Bottom-up: Chemical synthesis from molecular precursors for atomic precision (great for
electronics).
Lithography/cutting from larger graphene sheets.
These align with our open-source, buildable ethos—unzipping could integrate directly with our CNT
supply chains for POL v0.1/v0.2 refinements.

Properties (Grounded & Exciting)
Mechanical: Ultra-high tensile strength (comparable or better than CNTs in composites),
flexibility, and toughness. Studies show GNR-epoxy composites dramatically improve Young's
modulus and load transfer at low loadings (e.g., 0.3 wt%).
Electrical: Tunable bandgap based on width/edge type (armchair vs. zigzag)—enabling
transistors, sensors, and spintronics. Excellent conductivity for electrodes or resonant circuits.
Thermal: Superior conductivity; potential for thermal rectification or harvesting.
Other: High surface area, chemical tunability (e.g., functionalization for bio-compatibility), and
energy storage potential (e.g., Li-ion anodes).

Honest limitations: Scalable production with precise edge control remains challenging (defects
affect properties); dispersion in matrices can be tricky without functionalization; cost is still higher

than bulk materials, though dropping.

Yes — orbital paths become much clearer once field lines and the associated pressure terms
are in the picture.

In the solar corona and solar wind, the dominant “orbital” behavior of individual charged
particles is guiding-center motion along magnetic field lines:

     Particles gyrate in tight helices around a field line (the gyro-radius is usually tiny compared
     with the large-scale structures).

     The guiding center itself streams parallel to the field line.

     The result is that the particles are effectively tied to the field lines and follow their geometry.

The large-scale topology therefore dictates the paths:

     Open field lines (coronal holes) allow particles to escape and form the fast solar wind.

     Closed field lines (loops, streamers) trap plasma until reconnection or other processes
     open a path.

     Solar rotation winds the outward-advected field into the Parker spiral, so the asymptotic
     particle trajectories in the ecliptic become Archimedean spirals rather than straight radial
     lines.

Pressure gradients enter as the driving force that accelerates the plasma along those field lines.
The total pressure (gas + magnetic) sets the expansion speed and the location of the critical
(sonic/Alfvén) points. In other words, the field lines supply the “tracks,” while the pressure
gradients supply the “engine” that determines how fast material moves along those tracks.

At the fluid (MHD) level we speak of streamlines rather than individual orbits. Those streamlines
still follow the frozen-in field lines in ideal MHD, and the pressure-gradient + magnetic-tension
forces determine the velocity field. Steep pressure gradients appear at co-rotating interaction
regions and at the boundaries of coronal mass ejections, producing observable deflections and
compressions of the flow paths.

Once the magnetic topology is known (or extrapolated from photospheric magnetograms) and
the pressure is constrained by density/temperature diagnostics or in-situ data:

     Particle trajectories and bulk streamlines become predictable within the limits of the model
     (potential-field source-surface, nonlinear force-free, or full MHD).
     Parker Solar Probe and Solar Orbiter now sample these paths directly, confirming that
     particles largely follow the local field while the overall expansion is pressure-driven.

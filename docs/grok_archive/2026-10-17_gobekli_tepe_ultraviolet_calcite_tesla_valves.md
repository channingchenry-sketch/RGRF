Second-Harmonic Generation (SHG) in Calcite
SHG (also called frequency doubling) is a nonlinear optical process: two photons of frequency ω
interact in a nonlinear medium to produce one photon of 2ω (half the wavelength, double the
energy/frequency). It requires a non-zero second-order nonlinear susceptibility (χ²), which is

forbidden in centrosymmetric (inversion-symmetric) media under the electric-dipole approximation.

In calcite specifically:
Ideal bulk calcite is centrosymmetric, so bulk SHG is expected to be weak or absent. However,
natural microcrystals (especially in biological contexts) show clear SHG.
Landmark studies (Baconnier et al., 2002 and follow-ups) identified calcite microcrystals in the
human pineal gland and demonstrated they are the source of previously observed SHG signals in
pineal tissue sections. The complex texture and morphology (e.g., hexagonal or irregular forms)
likely break local symmetry, enabling the effect.
Similar findings in otoconia (calcite-based inner-ear crystals): SHG microscopy shows stronger
signals from otoconia than from pure calcite reference crystals in some comparisons, again
attributed to structural features.

Tesla Valve as a Fluidic Diode — Core Principles
A Tesla valve (valvular conduit) is a passive, fixed-geometry fluidic diode or check valve. It allows
fluid (liquid or gas) to flow with low resistance in the forward direction while creating high resistance
in the reverse direction — all without any moving parts.
How it works:
Forward flow: Fluid travels smoothly through the main channel. Side loops or “buckets” are
largely bypassed or contribute minimally to resistance (mostly surface friction).
Reverse flow: Fluid is diverted into the side loops. This creates turbulence, vortices, and
colliding flow streams that interfere with the incoming fluid, dramatically increasing pressure
drop and resistance.
The result is diodic behavior — analogous to an electronic diode but for fluids. Tesla described
it as offering “virtually no resistance” forward while constituting “an almost impassable barrier”
in reverse.
Key performance metric: Diodicity (D)
D = (pressure drop in reverse flow) / (pressure drop in forward flow) at the same volumetric flow rate.
Higher D = better diode performance. Typical values range from ~2–5 for simple designs to 15–40+
(or higher) in optimized multi-stage versions.

Performance Factors (from research)

Number of stages (loops): More stages generally increase diodicity. Multi-stage designs (e.g.,
4–10 stages) show significantly better rectification.
Reynolds number (Re): Performance improves at higher Re (transitional to turbulent flow helps
the reverse-flow interference and vortex formation). Optimized designs can still work well at
lower Re typical of microfluidics.
Pulsatile / oscillating flow: Strongly enhances diodicity. Studies confirm Tesla’s original intuition
— the valve performs better with reversing or unsteady flows than steady flow. This is highly
relevant for life-support systems with variable demand or decomposition pulses.
Geometry optimization: Loop angles, channel shapes (rectangular vs. circular), and feedback
angles affect performance. Modern CFD and 3D-printed prototypes have produced improved
versions.
Scale: Works from microfluidics (lab-on-chip, precise dosing) to macro scales (pipes,
propulsion). 3D printing makes custom fabrication straightforward in various materials (plastics,
ceramics, metals).
Advantages for life support:
No moving parts → Extremely reliable, low maintenance, resistant to wear/fatigue — critical in
closed-loop systems where failure is unacceptable.
Passive operation → No power required for the diode function itself.
Robust to pulsatile flows → Handles variable gas/liquid flows from HOOH decomposition or crew
demand.
Scalable and fabricable → Easy to integrate or 3D-print as part of modular nodes.

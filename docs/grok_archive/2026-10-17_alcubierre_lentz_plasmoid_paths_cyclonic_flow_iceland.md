What Is a Spheromak?
A spheromak is a compact toroid plasma configuration with both poloidal (looping in the poloidal
plane) and toroidal (around the major axis) magnetic fields, generated entirely by internal plasma
currents. Unlike tokamaks, it has no central solenoid or hole — it is “simply connected.” The
equilibrium is often near force-free:

∇ × B = λB
where λ is related to the magnetic helicity (a topological measure of field-line linkage). This selforganization allows the plasma to confine itself with relatively simple external coils or walls.
Spheromaks form via helicity injection (e.g., coaxial guns or inductive methods) and can relax toward
minimum-energy states while conserving helicity (Taylor relaxation).

Confinement Stability Investigation
Ideal MHD stability (early foundational work, e.g., Rosenbluth & Bussac 1979):
Spheromaks can achieve stable betas of at least a few percent in ideal MHD for certain aspect ratios
and shapes. However, they are particularly vulnerable to the tilt instability (a rigid-body tilting of
the plasma column in the confining field, often n=1, m=1 mode). Shift modes and kink modes can
also appear. A nearby conducting wall provides stabilization by image currents, but this introduces
new challenges.
Resistive wall modes (RWM) and resistive effects:
When the wall has finite conductivity, the stabilizing image currents decay, allowing slowly growing
RWMs. These are a long-standing issue across many configurations, including spheromaks. Plasma
rotation or sheared flows can help stabilize RWMs by Doppler shifting or averaging out the mode.
Pressure-driven instabilities:
At higher beta (plasma pressure relative to magnetic pressure), interchange and ballooning modes
become important. These limit achievable beta; low-n modes generally have the strongest impact on
confinement. Extended MHD (two-fluid effects, drifts, Hall terms) can stabilize high-n modes in
some regimes while having mixed or destabilizing effects on low-n modes.
Experimental observations (key devices):
SSPX (Sustained Spheromak Physics Experiment, LLNL): Demonstrated formation and some
sustainment via helicity injection. Discharges often lasted ~10–12 ms before terminating due to
MHD instabilities linked to peaking current profiles. Multi-pulse techniques or profile control
helped extend performance in some shots.

HIT-SI (Helicity Injected Torus – Steady Inductive): Uses steady inductive helicity injection for
better sustainment and control. Achieved improved stability and longer-lived configurations
compared to gun-driven methods in some regimes by maintaining more uniform current profiles
and helicity.
General trend: Spheromaks form readily and can be sustained for milliseconds to tens of
milliseconds, but global MHD modes (especially tilt/shift) and resistive decay limit performance.
Beta is typically modest (a few percent) before instabilities degrade confinement.
Mitigation and control strategies explored in literature:
Conducting or close-fitting walls for ideal mode stabilization.
Plasma rotation and flow shear to stabilize RWMs and some pressure-driven modes.
Helicity injection methods (steady inductive preferred for reduced impurities and better profile
control).
Extended MHD effects and drifts that can reduce growth rates of certain modes.
Multi-pulse or active profile control to avoid current peaking that triggers instabilities.
Recent theoretical work on tilting modes using 3-D MHD/PIC simulations confirms the need for
careful wall proximity and flow effects.
In short: Spheromaks demonstrate beautiful self-organization and positive-energy confinement, but
they are not yet a “solved” configuration for high-performance, long-pulse operation. Stability is
achievable at modest parameters with wall proximity and careful driving, yet global modes and
resistive effects remain limiting factors.

additional transport equations.

Boundary conditions: Inlet velocity/pressure, wall no-slip, outlet pressure.
Outputs: Velocity vectors/streamlines (helical paths), pressure contours (low-pressure core),
turbulence intensity, particle trajectories.
Common software: ANSYS Fluent (widely used for cyclones with DPM), OpenFOAM (open-source),
COMSOL, or Star-CCM+.

Strong axial and tangential velocities creating the vortex.
Radial pressure gradient sustaining centrifugal separation.
Turbulent fluctuations affecting mixing and wall interactions.
Particle separation efficiency vs. flow rate, geometry, and inlet conditions.

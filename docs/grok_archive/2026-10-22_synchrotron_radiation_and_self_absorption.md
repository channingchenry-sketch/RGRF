Quick Grounded Assessment
It's a useful intuition but not fully accurate in the strict physics sense. Synchrotron radiation
(from relativistic charged particles spiraling in magnetic fields) is indeed a powerhouse process
that generates broadband electromagnetic emission across a huge range—from
radio/microwaves up through optical, X-rays, and even gamma rays, depending on electron
energies and field strengths.
Synchrotron self-absorption (SSA) is the natural counterpart: at low frequencies (where the
source becomes optically thick), the same relativistic electrons that emit the radiation also
absorb it. This leads to:
A characteristic low-frequency spectral shape (often ~ν^{5/2} for a simple power-law
electron distribution in the optically thick regime, or modified in real inhomogeneous
sources).
A turnover frequency where the spectrum transitions from absorbed (rising) to the optically
thin power-law decline.
It prevents the brightness temperature from exceeding physical limits and shapes the
observed spectrum, especially in astrophysical sources like radio supernovae, AGN jets, or
supernova remnants.
Together, emission + self-absorption beautifully describe the full observed spectrum from a
single synchrotron source across its relevant bands—low frequencies dominated by absorption
effects, higher by emission. This is a key tool in modeling real cosmic plasmas.
However, it doesn't literally equal the "full EM scale" (entire electromagnetic spectrum)
in isolation:
Other mechanisms (thermal bremsstrahlung, dust emission, atomic/molecular lines,
Compton scattering, etc.) fill gaps or dominate in different regimes/contexts.
Synchrotron itself can extend very broadly but has practical cutoffs based on particle
energy, magnetic field, and environment. SSA mainly "fills in" or modifies the low end rather
than magically completing everything.
In short: Strong partial truth for understanding compact, magnetized plasma sources—

The sewing machine emerged during the First Industrial Revolution to move clothing and fabric

Lockstitch revolution: Walter Hunt developed an early American lockstitch idea (1830s). Elias

needle with the eye at the point, a shuttle for the second thread, and automatic feed. This
became the foundational design.

How the Core Mechanism Works (Lockstitch)
Two threads interlock securely:
1. The needle (with upper thread) pierces the fabric and forms a loop on the underside.
2. A shuttle or rotary hook carries the lower thread (from a bobbin) through that loop.
3. The threads lock together in the middle of the fabric layers.
This creates strong, balanced seams that don’t unravel easily and can form anywhere on the
material. Variants like chain stitch (one thread, looper) or overlock (edge finishing with trimming)
serve different purposes.

Modern Evolution & Technology

Today’s machines range from basic mechanical to highly sophisticated computerized models:
Stepper motors and electronics enable precise needle positioning, automated tension, thread
cutting, and programmable patterns.
Embroidery/combination machines use hoops or arms moved by computer control for complex
designs; many feature WiFi, app integration, touchscreens, laser guides, and even built-in
cameras for previewing stitches on fabric.
Industrial versions handle heavy-duty, high-speed work on technical textiles, upholstery,
composites preforms, or specialized materials. Automated systems support consistent,
repeatable joining for production-scale output.

Biological Foundation: Nature’s Zippering in Mycelium-Lignocellulose
Systems
Mycelium-based composites (MBCs) are essentially a living “zippering” process:
Chitin in mycelium: Fungal hyphae have cell walls rich in chitin (a tough polysaccharide). Chitin
nanofibrils and crystalline fibers are often organized in zipper-like structures — chains bound
tightly via hydrogen bonding and dipole interactions, creating strong, stable nano-assemblies.
This provides mechanical integrity and flexibility to the mycelial network.
Lignin in hemp: Hemp is a lignocellulosic substrate (cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin).
Mycelium (especially white-rot fungi like Trametes versicolor) partially decomposes lignin for
nutrients while its hyphal network physically intertwines, penetrates, and binds the fibers

surface area, but can convert to other forms.

Yes — this is a strong match for the zipper idea we explored earlier.

Recent work (published August/September 2026 in Nucleic Acids Research) from teams at the
University of Sheffield and University of York has given the first direct images and simulations of
two DNA double helices locking together groove-for-groove, exactly as the long-standing
“electrostatic DNA zipper” model predicted.

What they actually saw
Researchers used high-resolution atomic force microscopy on short DNA fragments (in liquid,
on mica) and combined it with molecular dynamics simulations. Two already-formed double
helices sat side-by-side with their major and minor grooves aligned like the teeth of a zip.
Positively charged divalent metal ions (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, and especially Ni²⁺ for highest resolution)
form bridges that nestle into the grooves and hold the negatively charged backbones together
despite the expected electrostatic repulsion.

Key observations:

     Grooves match groove-for-groove (major with major, minor with minor).
     Contacts typically last 3–4 helical turns (~14–16 nm).

     Certain sequences (notably GTAC with nickel ions) form especially stable bridges.

     Pairing can start even without perfect sequence matching; ion bridges initiate local contact,
     then sequence homology can extend and stabilize the “zip.”

This confirms the model first proposed around 1999–2001 by Alexey Kornyshev and
collaborators (the electrostatic zipper / helical coherence idea), in which counterions bound in
the grooves create alternating charge patterns that allow neighboring helices to interlock.

How this connects to what we discussed
We previously framed DNA as a zipper in the context of both replication (helicase as the

molecular unzipper) and the deeper structural/informational properties of the double helix —
including its crystalline packing tendencies and geometric regularities. This new work is
specifically about inter-helical pairing (two duplexes zipping alongside each other), which is
relevant to homologous recognition, recombination, DNA packaging inside the nucleus, and
certain gene-silencing or cancer-related processes. It is not the classic strand-separation
zipper of replication, but it extends the same mechanical/electrostatic intuition.

The “two-sided” aspect also resonates with the double-stranded, antiparallel nature of DNA
itself and with ideas about information density and dual reading frames. The structural
confirmation of precise groove alignment strengthens the picture of DNA as a highly ordered,
self-organizing lattice rather than a simple string of bases.

Resonance with our work (phi, geometry, regenerative systems)

alignment adds another layer of ordered electrostatic patterning that can be sequence-

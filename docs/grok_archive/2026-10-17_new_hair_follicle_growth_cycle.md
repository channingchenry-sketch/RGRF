==== [2] GROK ====
Yes — let’s walk through it carefully.

A “new” hair follicle can mean two related but distinct processes:

1. True de novo formation (morphogenesis) that occurs mainly during embryonic development,
   and
2. Cyclic regeneration of an existing follicle in the adult, which produces a new hair shaft from the
    same structure.

True postnatal neogenesis (brand-new follicles appearing where none existed) is rare in humans
under normal conditions, though limited wound-induced hair neogenesis has been demonstrated in
mice and is an active research area for regenerative medicine.

Embryonic Hair Follicle Morphogenesis (True New Follicle Formation)
This is a classic example of epithelial–mesenchymal interaction. It unfolds in coordinated stages:

Induction

    A still-not-fully-identified “first dermal signal” (upstream of widespread Wnt activity in the
    upper dermis) causes clusters of epidermal cells to thicken into placodes.

    The placode then signals back (key molecule: FGF20) so that underlying dermal fibroblasts
    migrate and aggregate into a dermal condensate.

Organogenesis

    The placode grows downward as a hair germ / hair peg.

    The dermal condensate becomes enclosed and matures into the dermal papilla (the permanent
    mesenchymal signaling center).

    Sonic hedgehog (Shh) signaling becomes critical for further downgrowth.

Cytodifferentiation

    Matrix keratinocytes surrounding the dermal papilla proliferate and differentiate into the
    concentric layers of the hair shaft and inner root sheath.

    The outer root sheath, bulge (future stem-cell niche), sebaceous gland, and arrector pili muscle
    form.

    Melanocytes from the neural crest populate the bulb to pigment the hair.

Key conserved pathways throughout: Wnt/β-catenin, Shh, BMP (often inhibitory for spacing),
Eda/Edar, FGF, and Notch. Spacing of follicles is largely achieved by lateral inhibition (BMP and Dkk
family members) so that placodes do not form too close together.

In humans this process begins around week 9–12 of gestation (cephalocaudal wave) and is
essentially complete by ~22 weeks. After birth, the total number of follicles is largely fixed.

Adult Hair Cycle — How a New Hair Shaft Grows from an Existing Follicle
Once formed, each follicle cycles repeatedly:

    Anagen (growth): Stem cells in the bulge are activated by signals from the dermal papilla (FGF7,
    Noggin, etc.). The lower follicle regenerates downward, matrix cells proliferate rapidly, and a new
    hair shaft is produced. Scalp anagen can last 2–6+ years.

    Catagen (regression): Controlled apoptosis shortens the lower follicle; the dermal papilla is
    drawn upward.

    Telogen (rest): Quiescent phase.
    Exogen (shedding): Old club hair is released, and the follicle can re-enter anagen.

Recent live-imaging work (2026) has refined the mechanics: the classic textbook picture of pure
“push from below” is incomplete. Coordinated downward spiral migration of cells in the outer root
sheath generates a pulling force that helps draw the shaft upward — essentially a cellular motor in
addition to basal proliferation.

Regenerative Edge Cases
Under specific conditions (large full-thickness wounds in mice), adult skin can reactivate
embryonic-like programs and form new follicles (wound-induced hair neogenesis). This requires re-
activation of Wnt and Shh pathways and involves both interfollicular and follicular stem cells.
Translating this reliably to human skin remains a major research goal.

The “Pulling Motor” Findings (Deeper Look)
The classic textbook model said matrix cells at the base of the follicle divide rapidly and simply push
the hair shaft upward. A 2025 Nature Communications study (Tissot, Sequeira, Bornschlögl and
colleagues from L’Oréal Research & Innovation + Queen Mary University of London) overturned that
picture with high-resolution 3D live imaging of human anagen follicles kept alive in culture.

Key observations:

    Cells in the outer root sheath (ORS) — the outer epithelial layer surrounding the hair shaft —
    move in a coordinated spiral-like downward path as they enter the lower bulb region.
    At the same time, inner layers (companion layer, inner root sheath/IRS, and cortex) flow upward,
    but at different speeds (IRS often faster than cortex).

    When researchers blocked mitosis (cell division) in the matrix, hair continued growing at nearly
    normal rates. This directly contradicted a pure “push-from-below” model.
    When they interfered with actin (the cytoskeletal protein that enables cells to generate
    contractile force and move), growth rate dropped by more than 80 %.

They combined these experiments with fluid-dynamics simulations that treat the living tissue as a
viscous continuum. The only models that reproduced the measured velocity profiles required an
active pulling force generated by the ORS acting on the adjacent companion layer (and thereby on
the IRS and cortex). In short, the ORS functions like a microscopic motor: its downward
migratory/contractile activity creates a shear force that draws the inner differentiating layers (and
thus the forming hair fiber) upward.

This is still an active-force contribution in addition to matrix proliferation; it does not eliminate the
need for new cells. It simply shows that mechanical traction from the outer layers is a major driver
of extrusion during anagen. The finding opens practical doors for hair-loss research (targeting
cytoskeletal or migratory dynamics) and for tissue-engineering efforts that try to recreate
functional follicles.

Hair Standing Up — Piloerection and Alertness
The mechanism that makes hair stand on end is completely separate from the growth motor above. It
is produced by the arrector pili muscles — small bundles of smooth muscle that attach from the
dermal connective tissue to the hair follicle, typically near the bulge (the stem-cell niche region).

When these muscles contract, they:

    Pull the follicle into a more upright position,

    Cause the skin surface to dimple into the familiar “goosebump,”

    And, in furred animals, trap a layer of insulating air or make the animal appear larger.

Control is sympathetic (autonomic). Post-ganglionic fibers release norepinephrine onto α1-
adrenergic receptors on the smooth-muscle cells, triggering contraction. This is the same branch of
the nervous system that orchestrates the broader fight-or-flight / heightened-alertness response.

Triggers relevant to “increased alertness or awareness”:

    Cold (classic thermoregulatory role).

    Perceived threat or fear (makes many mammals look bigger and more intimidating).

    Strong emotional or aesthetic arousal (music that gives chills, awe, sudden insight, intense
    focus).

    General sympathetic activation linked to vigilance and readiness.

In humans the insulating and visual-display functions are largely vestigial because of our sparse
body hair, yet the reflex remains tightly coupled to states of heightened arousal and sensory
awareness. Recent work also shows that the arrector pili + sympathetic innervation forms a dual
niche that can influence hair-follicle stem-cell activity, so the same pathway that raises the hair can,
under certain conditions, modulate regenerative potential of the follicle itself.

In animals that can regenerate tails limbs appendages is there this stem cell activity is the trigger more basal related?

==== [6] GROK ====
Yes — there is stem/progenitor cell activity, but the picture is broader and more plastic than the
tightly niche-regulated stem cells we see in mammalian hair follicles.

Cellular Sources in Regenerative Appendages
Animals that regenerate tails, limbs, or fins (axolotls/newts, lizards, zebrafish, and some other
amphibians/fish) form a blastema — a proliferative mass of progenitor cells at the injury site that
rebuilds the missing structure. The cells that populate the blastema come from two main routes
(often combined, and varying by species, life stage, and tissue):

    Resident stem/progenitor cells — These are activated by injury. Classic examples include Pax7+
    muscle satellite cells (major source of new muscle in axolotl limbs), ependymal/neural stem cells
    along the spinal cord (critical for tail regeneration), periosteal progenitors for skeletal elements,
    and various connective-tissue progenitors. In lizard tails, Pax7+ satellite cells and other slowly
    cycling resident cells also contribute.

    Dedifferentiation — Mature, specialized cells (e.g., myofibers in some newts, certain fibroblasts
    or osteoblasts) lose their differentiated character, re-enter the cell cycle, and contribute
    progenitors. This is more prominent in some contexts than pure resident-stem-cell activation.
    Lineage-tracing studies generally show that cells remain largely restricted to their original germ-
    layer or tissue type rather than becoming fully pluripotent.

Recent single-cell work confirms the blastema is heterogeneous and context-dependent: different
appendages (limb vs. tail) and different species recruit different mixes of these cells. There is no
single universal “regeneration stem cell.”

Triggers — More Basal / Injury-Driven
The trigger is primarily injury itself, not a cyclic, niche-timed activation like the hair-follicle bulge.
Key elements include:

    Local wound signals (reactive oxygen species, mechanical disruption of the extracellular matrix,
    immune-cell recruitment).

    Formation of a specialized wound epidermis that signals to underlying cells.

    Nerve dependence in many classic cases (especially salamander limbs): denervation blocks
    blastema formation and outgrowth. Nerves supply neurotrophic factors (FGFs, BMPs,
    neuregulins, nAG/Prod1 interactions, etc.). This is a more systemic, evolutionarily ancient
    coupling of innervation to regeneration.

    Reactivation of embryonic developmental programs (Wnt, FGF, Shh, BMP, TGF-β pathways) with
    positional information from the remaining stump so the regenerate matches the lost level.

In this sense the process is “more basal”: it re-uses and expands developmental plasticity that
mammals largely restrict after embryonic stages. Hair-follicle cycling is a highly specialized, ongoing
mini-regeneration that stays within a stable adult niche. Full appendage regeneration is a more
dramatic, injury-triggered return toward embryonic-like potency and patterning.

Honest Limitations and Links
Regenerative capacity is ancient and widespread among vertebrates, yet comparative genomics
finds little evidence of a single conserved “regeneration gene set” (Wnt family signaling is one
recurrent theme). Differences between salamanders (near-perfect limb/tail regeneration) and lizards
(cartilaginous, less patterned tails) already show how stem-cell identity and signaling diverge.

This sits interestingly next to the hair-follicle work we discussed: both systems rely on epithelial–
mesenchymal interactions, stem/progenitor activation, and cytoskeletal/mechanical forces, but the
scale, trigger, and cellular plasticity differ markedly. Insights from blastema biology continue to
inform efforts to enhance limited mammalian regeneration (digit tips, antlers, etc.).

In antlers there’s a significant increase in progesterone, right is that the inhibiting factor there?

==== [8] GROK ====
No — progesterone is not the main inhibiting factor.

In the annual antler cycle of deer (cervids), the dominant endocrine control comes from androgens
(especially testosterone) and, at the cellular level, often estrogen/estradiol. Progesterone plays a
secondary or indirect role at best.

Core Pattern
    Low / near-undetectable testosterone permits casting of the previous hard antler and initiates
    regeneration + rapid velvet antler growth. This is the permissive window for the pedicle
    periosteum stem/progenitor cells to activate and form the blastema-like growth center.

    Rising and high testosterone (late summer into the rut) stops longitudinal growth, drives
    mineralization/ossification, causes velvet shedding, and maintains the hard antler phase. High
    levels act as a brake.

    Estrogen/estradiol is frequently more potent than testosterone at the local cellular level.
    Exogenous estrogen strongly inhibits progenitor-cell proliferation in the growing tip while
    promoting differentiation and premature ossification. Many researchers consider estrogen a key
    “brake” hormone even in males (antler tissue itself can aromatize androgens into estrogen).

Progesterone appears in seasonal blood profiles (especially in females and during pregnancy in
reindeer/caribou), but studies do not identify a significant natural rise in progesterone as the primary
inhibitor of antler growth or regeneration. In males its fluctuations are not tightly coupled to the
growth–ossification transition the way testosterone and estrogen are.

Experimental Notes on Progesterone / Progestins
    Synthetic progestins such as medroxyprogesterone acetate (MPA) or melengestrol acetate
    (MGA) are sometimes given to farmed stags to suppress aggressive rutting behavior. They work
    mainly by suppressing the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis (lowering LH and thus
    testosterone).

    This secondary drop in testosterone can cause premature casting or prolong the growth phase
    in some experiments, but the effect is indirect.

    Older work comparing hormones in castrated stags found that progesterone could mimic some
    testosterone effects (inducing cleaning/shedding) but was 2–3 times less potent.

    In female reindeer, elevated progesterone during pregnancy coincides with retention of hard
    antlers, yet estrogen is still regarded as the more important regulator of the hard-antler state.

In short, the significant hormonal “off switch” for continued antler growth and the trigger that locks
the structure into the hard phase is the seasonal rise in testosterone (with important local
contributions from estrogen). Progesterone is present and can influence the system indirectly or in
specific experimental contexts, but it is not the primary inhibiting factor.

Investigate antler regeneration mechanisms

==== [10] GROK ====
Deer antler regeneration is the only known example of complete, annual, stem-cell-based organ
regeneration in a postnatal mammal. It is a highly regulated epimorphic process that does not rely
primarily on classical dedifferentiation of mature cells (as in salamander limbs), but on the periodic
activation of specialized periosteal stem/progenitor cells.

Core Cellular Basis
Three main populations drive the process:

    Antlerogenic periosteum (AP) cells — Form the pedicle and the first set of antlers during
    puberty. These are the foundational stem cells.

   Pedicle periosteum (PP) cells (also called antler stem cells or PPCs) — Reside in the permanent
   pedicle. They are essential for annual regeneration. Complete surgical removal of the PP
   abolishes regeneration; partial removal shifts the growth center to the remaining PP tissue.
   These cells express both mesenchymal stem-cell markers (CD73, CD90, CD105) and key
   embryonic markers (Oct4, Nanog, SOX2, TERT, nucleostemin). They are multipotent
   (chondrogenic, osteogenic, adipogenic, etc.) and self-renewing.

   Reserve mesenchyme cells (RMCs) — Located at the growing tip. These highly proliferative
   progenitors sustain the extraordinary elongation rate (up to ~2–2.75 cm per day).

Recent single-cell work has identified antler blastema progenitor cells (ABPCs) that arise from
PRRX1⁺ mesenchymal cells and act as a key directing population during regeneration.

Sequence of Events
1. Casting — Triggered by the seasonal drop in circulating testosterone (linked to increasing day
   length). The previous hard antler is shed from the pedicle.

2. Wound healing — The exposed pedicle stump heals. Full-thickness pedicle skin migrates
   inward. Systemic circulating factors (elevated during the antler regeneration period) support
   regenerative-style cutaneous healing even if local PP is absent, but full antler formation requires
   the PP.

3. Growth-center formation — PP cells proliferate and form distinct anterior (brow tine) and
   posterior (main beam) growth centers while wound healing is still completing. There is no
   classical multi-lineage blastema formed by dedifferentiation of all stump tissues.

4. Rapid elongation — Appositional growth from the tip via successive zones: reserve
   mesenchyme → pre-cartilage → cartilage → mineralizing cartilage → bone. Ossification is
   primarily endochondral, with evidence of hybrid features including possible direct hypertrophic
   chondrocyte-to-osteoblast transition.

5. Mineralization and velvet shedding — Rising testosterone (and local estrogen activity) drives
   calcification, cuts off blood supply to the velvet skin, and produces the hard antler used in the
   rut.

Molecular and Hormonal Control
Systemic triggers

   Low testosterone is permissive for casting and initiation. High testosterone (and estrogen) act as
   a “brake,” inhibiting progenitor proliferation and promoting differentiation/ossification.
   IGF-1 is a major circulating stimulator strongly correlated with growth rate. Other systemic
   contributors include GH, melatonin, and factors such as PRG4.

Local signaling (recapitulating many embryonic limb and growth-plate pathways)

   Canonical Wnt/β-catenin

   FGF, BMP, TGF-β families

   PTHrP–Indian Hedgehog axis

    PI3K/Akt and MAPK pathways

    Androgen–RXFP2 axis in specific periosteal subpopulations
    Calreticulin (CALR) — an androgen-responsive gene upregulated upon activation of the PP;
    appears important for initiating proliferation.

Immune cells (particularly macrophages) also play an instructive role. Recent work shows that
recruiting or injecting macrophages (or macrophage-attracting signals) can induce antler growth
even in female deer, indicating an immune–stem-cell niche interaction beyond pure steroid control.

Key Distinctions from Other Regenerative Systems
    Unlike salamander limb regeneration, antler regeneration does not require extensive
    dedifferentiation of mature differentiated cells; it is driven by activation of a resident periosteal
    stem-cell niche.
    Both local (PP-derived) and systemic factors are required for complete antler renewal. Systemic
    factors alone can support regenerative cutaneous healing.

    The process is tightly coupled to the reproductive cycle via sex steroids, yet the cellular
    machinery re-uses developmental skeletal pathways at an extreme scale and speed.

This system continues to yield practical insights: antler stem cells and their extracellular vesicles
show promise in promoting bone regeneration, reversing bone loss, and enhancing wound healing in
experimental models. The combination of a defined stem-cell niche, steroid gating, and rapid but
controlled skeletal growth makes antlers one of the most informative mammalian models for
regenerative biology.

RXFP2 or CALR mechanisms, comparisons to hair-follicle or salamander regeneration, or the
translational work with antler-derived cells/vesicles?

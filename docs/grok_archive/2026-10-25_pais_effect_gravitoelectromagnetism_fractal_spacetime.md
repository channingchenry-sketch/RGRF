Fibroblast bioelectric core (grounded findings)
The 2025 Cell work (Chen et al.) starts from a rare human condition — congenital generalized
hypertrichosis terminalis (CGHT) — and extracts a general regenerative rule.

Structural variants on chromosome 17 disrupt a topologically associating domain (TAD). The
result is ectopic upregulation of KCNJ2 (Kir2.1, an inward-rectifying potassium channel)
specifically inside dermal fibroblasts. Other niche cells are unaffected.

Kir2.1 opening hyperpolarizes the fibroblast membrane. Hyperpolarization reduces calcium
influx. Lower intracellular Ca²⁺ increases the fibroblast’s responsiveness to Wnt/β-catenin
signals. The cells then secrete higher levels of paracrine drivers (RSPO3, SCUBE3 and related
factors) that instruct the hair follicle epithelium to enter and stay in anagen.

In the normal cycle the fibroblast membrane potential itself oscillates: more hyperpolarized
during growth phase, more depolarized during rest. Forced depolarization delays entry into
anagen; forced hyperpolarization produces continuous cycling and rescues both aging and
androgenetic-alopecia models in mice. The effect is cell-type specific — the same channel
change in other dermal cells does not drive the phenotype.

This is clean experimental biology. Membrane voltage is not a side-effect; it is an upstream
control knob that gates Wnt sensitivity and therefore the regenerative decision of the entire
follicle niche.

### Message 10 — Grok (all figures Grok-reported)

Yes — piezoelectric and electrolytes sit right at the heart of the mild, self-powered voltage
domains we are discussing.

In a living or hydrated mycelium scaffold the two work together rather than against each other.

Two distinct mechanisms
Piezoelectric (lattice polarization): Mechanical stress displaces bound charges in a crystal
lattice or ordered polymer, producing a voltage. Classic materials are ceramics (BaTiO₃, etc.) or
piezoelectric polymers (PLLA, PVDF). Output can be relatively high (tens to hundreds of mV or
more under ultrasound or stronger deformation), but pure piezoelectric phases are often stiffer.

Piezoionic (ion redistribution): Stress creates a transient concentration gradient of mobile ions
inside a soft, electrolyte-filled network. Different mobilities of cations and anions generate a
voltage and ionic current. Outputs are typically lower (1–500 mV range) but the materials are far
softer, more compliant, and the signal is carried by ions — exactly the same currency cells use
for membrane potential and intercellular communication.

Recent reviews of electroactive hydrogels treat these as complementary platforms.
Piezoelectric hydrogels often embed piezoelectric particles or nanofibers in a soft matrix;
piezoionic hydrogels rely on the polymer network + mobile ions themselves. Both can be
self-powered.

Why electrolytes matter here
In any hydrated biological or mycelial system the extracellular and interstitial fluid is
an electrolyte. Ions screen charges, enable conduction, and are the actual carriers of

bioelectric signals.

Mycelium is already an excellent ionic host. Natural hyphal walls carry negative surface charge
(roughly −1.8 to −2.8 mC m⁻², tunable by growth time). In dilute KCl or similar electrolytes the
interconnected nanochannels show ionic conductivities of 0.2–0.5 mS cm⁻¹ — orders of
magnitude above bulk values at low concentration. This is classic nanofluidic behavior. Living
mycelium further supports ionic signal propagation that collapses when the material is
dehydrated and recovers on rehydration.

So the electrolyte is not a problem to be engineered around; it is the working medium. A
piezoelectric inclusion or an ordered peptide/polymer phase can generate an initial polarization;
the surrounding ionic fluid and mycelial nanochannels then distribute and sustain a local field
that can bias nearby fibroblasts toward hyperpolarization.

Practical fit for our scaffolds
Mild pathways without continuous high power: Embed sparse piezoelectric nanofibers
(e.g., short PLLA) or intrinsically polar peptide gels inside the mycelium matrix, or simply
exploit the mycelium’s own ionic network for piezoionic response. Mechanical cues already
present in the system — growth stresses, low-frequency vibration, gentle ultrasound, or
even ambient movement — generate transient local voltages.

Reinforcing hyperpolarization domains: The generated ionic currents or fields sit in the
same amplitude window already shown to influence fibroblast membrane potential and
electrotaxis (tens to a few hundred mV mm⁻¹). Because the signal is ionic and local, it can
help hold the desired anagen-like state across larger areas without a continuous external
supply.

Compatibility with living hybrids: Both piezoelectric and piezoionic approaches work in
hydrated, electrolyte-rich environments. Mycelium’s biocompatibility with fibroblasts
remains intact; the added electroactive phase simply supplies a passive or self-powered
bias.

Honest limits remain the same: long-term stability of the voltage domain in a growing, living
composite, quantitative mapping of local fields inside dense hyphal networks, and careful
matching of mechanical compliance so the scaffold does not become too stiff for soft-tissue
use. These are measurable engineering parameters, not fundamental obstacles.

Algae can still contribute as a parallel photosynthetic ion and oxygen source, further supporting
the electrolyte environment and possibly adding light-modulated membrane effects.

This combination — mycelium as hierarchical ionic host + mild piezoelectric or piezoionic
inclusions + fibroblast hyperpolarization bias — stays continuous with the regenerative principle
we are following: use the living continuum’s own mechanical and ionic physics rather than
imposing high-power external fields.

Ion displacement and distribution
At the heart of the piezoionic effect (and of membrane potential itself) is differential
ion movement.

When a soft, electrolyte-filled network (hydrogel, living mycelium, or hybrid scaffold)
experiences non-uniform mechanical stress:

1. A hydrostatic pressure (or fluid-flow) gradient appears.

2. Mobile ions are carried by that flow, but cations and anions have different mobilities and
different interactions with the polymer/hyphal matrix.
3. The faster species advances preferentially, creating a transient local concentration gradient
(and therefore a charge separation).

4. The resulting voltage (typically in the low-mV to hundreds-of-mV range) and ionic current
appear across the material or at electrode interfaces.

5. When the stress is released, ions diffuse back under the concentration and potential
gradients, restoring near-uniform distribution. The process is reversible and non-faradaic
under normal conditions.

Mycelium is especially well suited for this. Its hyphal walls carry a tunable negative surface
charge and form interconnected nanochannels. In dilute electrolytes these channels already
show elevated ionic conductivity (surface-mediated transport dominates at low bulk
concentrations). Mechanical deformation of the living or hydrated network can therefore drive
directed ion displacement along the same pathways that naturally support ion regulation.

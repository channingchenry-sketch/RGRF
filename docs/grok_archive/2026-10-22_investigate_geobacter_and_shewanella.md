Yes — Geobacter and Shewanella are excellent candidates for investigation. They are the two
best-characterized model genera of electrogenic (exoelectrogenic) bacteria, central to extracellular
electron transfer (EET), microbial fuel cells (MFCs), bioremediation, and emerging living materials /
bioelectronics. Their physiology aligns strongly with regenerative systems: converting organic waste
or contaminants into usable electrons or less-toxic forms, forming conductive biofilms and
nanowires, and potentially integrating with mycelium-based composites.
Core Physiology
Geobacter (e.g., G. sulfurreducens, G. metallireducens)
Strict anaerobes found in sediments, soils, and aquifers. They completely oxidize organic
compounds (including hydrocarbons) to CO₂ while reducing insoluble electron acceptors such as
Fe(III) oxides, Mn(IV), and U(VI). They form thick, highly conductive biofilms and were the first
organisms shown to perform direct electron transfer to electrodes.
Key mechanism: Direct EET via multiheme c-type cytochromes assembled into nanowires (primarily
OmcS and OmcZ filaments). These polymerized cytochrome nanowires enable long-range electron
transport (micrometers) with metallic-like conductivity in some measurements. Pili assist in
secretion and biofilm structure but are not the primary conductive filaments. They also engage in
direct interspecies electron transfer (DIET).
Shewanella (especially S. oneidensis MR-1)
Facultative anaerobes — more metabolically flexible. They use a wide range of electron acceptors
(oxygen, nitrate, fumarate, metals including Fe, Mn, Cr, U, and others).
Key mechanism: The Mtr pathway (CymA → periplasmic carriers → outer-membrane MtrCAB/OmcA
complex) supports both direct contact and mediated EET. They secrete flavins (riboflavin
and FMN) that act as electron shuttles; flavins can also bind as cofactors to outer-membrane
cytochromes, accelerating transfer. EET is bidirectional (they can both donate and accept electrons
from electrodes).

Established Applications
Microbial fuel cells and bioelectrochemical systems: Both generate current from organic waste
while treating wastewater. Geobacter-dominated communities often deliver the highest current
densities. Shewanella is frequently used for mechanistic studies and engineering because of its
growth rate and genetic tools. Recent work continues to push power densities via electrode
materials, genetic overexpression of cytochromes/flavins, and hybrid biofilms.
Bioremediation: Reduction of soluble U(VI), Cr(VI), and other metals to less mobile/insoluble
forms; anaerobic degradation of hydrocarbons and certain organics. Geobacter is particularly
noted for subsurface uranium and petroleum remediation.
Nanowires and bioelectronics: Geobacter cytochrome nanowires (OmcS/OmcZ) are being
explored for sustainable electronics — sensors, memristors/neuromorphic devices, and even
humidity-driven power generation from non-living biofilms. They are biodegradable, produced
from renewable feedstocks, and genetically malleable. Shewanella has been engineered to
express Geobacter OmcS filaments.
Electrosynthesis and other: Bidirectional EET enables cathode-driven reactions (e.g., reduction
of CO₂ or other acceptors). Both appear in mixed communities for resource recovery and
pollutant removal.
Relevance to Regenerative Planetary & Cosmic Infrastructure Work
These organisms fit naturally into bio-nodes, living materials, and distributed
energy/remediation layers:
Mycelium composites and living materials: Fungal filaments (including from edible or white-rot
species) have been used as anode precursors that enrich Geobacter and boost bio-capacitance
and power density. Broader fungal–bacterial living composites already exist and can be
engineered for sensing, self-repair, or remediation. Pairing Trametes-style mycelium (our
POL/RGRF material track) with electrogenic bacteria is a direct experimental avenue — bacteria
could supply electron pathways or redox activity inside or alongside mycelial matrices.
Energy harvesting from waste: Complementary to resonant wireless power or eddy-current
systems. MFCs or microbial electrolysis cells could recover energy from organic residues while
simultaneously cleaning water or soil — practical “breadcrumbs” at planetary scale.
Conductive living infrastructure: Cytochrome nanowires and conductive biofilms offer
biological analogs to engineered conductors. Potential for self-assembling, self-repairing
electronic or sensing elements within regenerative structures.
Planetary healing: In-situ metal immobilization, hydrocarbon cleanup, and redox balancing in
sediments or soils. Shewanella’s versatility makes it attractive for variable environments;
Geobacter’s high conductivity suits engineered high-performance nodes.
Honest limitations: Current densities remain far below conventional power systems (typically
mA/cm² range in optimized lab systems). Biofilms can develop internal pH or mass-transfer
constraints over long distances. Scale-up, long-term stability outside controlled conditions, and
integration with abiotic materials still require substantial engineering. Most high-performance
results are pure-culture or highly optimized lab systems; real environments introduce competition
and variability. Speculative extensions (e.g., integrating nanowires into phi-optimized or mycelium-
hemp structural elements, or using them in RGRF-style docking/energy nodes) are plausible
research directions but not yet demonstrated.

Not necessarily in that order — and the oxygen mismatch is the real constraint.
Mycelium (including the Trametes versicolor lineages we use with hemp) is primarily aerobic.
Geobacter is a strict anaerobe. Shewanella is facultative and therefore much easier to hybridize. That
changes the sequencing logic.
What the literature actually shows
Direct living hybrid: Trametes versicolor + Shewanella oneidensis has already been run in an
MFC. The bacteria used the fungal hyphal network as a conductive/transport scaffold to reach
the anode; power output rose and the system stayed stable.
Fungus-first scaffold (non-living): Carbonized filament-array anodes made from edible fungi
(Flammulina, Pleurotus) strongly enrich living Geobacter (up to ~45 %) and deliver high power
densities (~3.5 W m⁻²) with excellent bio-capacitance.
Mycelium + other bacteria: Co-cultures of mycelium with bacteria (e.g., Ganoderma +
Sporosarcina for microbially induced calcium carbonate precipitation) are established and can
improve mechanical properties. The fungal network acts as a transport highway for the bacteria.
Cellulose/lignocellulose feeding: Ternary systems (cellulose degraders + Geobacter) and
Shewanella + Cellulomonas consortia successfully generate current from lignocellulosic
substrates, showing the substrate can be shared or pre-processed.

## Message 8 — Grok

_(worked for 11s)_

Using them together already improves performance, and a quadrant (or layered) design fits their
natural division of labor.
What happens in mixed culture
Defined co-cultures of Geobacter sulfurreducens and Shewanella oneidensis have been tested in
bioelectrochemical systems. The clearest long-term data come from Engel et al. (2019):
Pure Geobacter: ~0.39 mA cm⁻², ~69 μm biofilm
Pure Shewanella: ~0.034 mA cm⁻², weak biofilm
Defined mixed culture (acetate + lactate): ~0.54 mA cm⁻² (≈38 % higher than Geobacter alone)
and a thicker biofilm (~93 μm)
The benefit is real, but the mechanism is specific: Shewanella was not stably locked into the mature
biofilm. The performance gain came mainly from the presence of planktonic Shewanella cells, which
improved Geobacter biofilm thickness and current. When planktonic cells were removed, the
advantage declined.
More recent microfluidic co-culture work shows a spatial pattern that is useful for design:
Roughly two-thirds of the biofilm is Geobacter forming a continuous conductive 3D network.
Shewanella appears as embedded tubular colonies inside that matrix, often without direct
contact to the electrode.
Cross-feeding occurs: Shewanella converts lactate to acetate, which Geobacter then uses.
There is a working hypothesis of direct interspecies electron transfer (DIET) in which Shewanella
may hand electrons to the Geobacter conductive matrix.

A quadrant or zoned layout on a substrate (hemp, bamboo, wool) could intentionally create these
niches instead of relying on random mixing:
1. Outer / edge quadrants — Shewanella (tolerates residual oxygen, produces flavins, can process
a wider range of organics from the substrate or mycelium breakdown).
2. Inner / protected quadrants — Geobacter (anaerobic core, high-conductivity biofilm, benefits
from acetate supplied by Shewanella).
3. Mycelium network — Structural scaffold that can also serve as a physical bridge between the
zones (similar to how Trametes hyphae have already been shown to help Shewanella reach an
electrode).
This is closer to a living “circuit board” than a uniform mixture. It also reduces the risk of Shewanella
being outcompeted or of oxygen killing Geobacter.
Practical notes for a hybrid material
Start with Shewanella in the more aerobic zones and introduce Geobacter only after anaerobic
pockets form (mycelium density + sealing can help create those pockets).
Supply a mixed carbon source (lactate + acetate or a lignocellulosic hydrolysate) so cross-
feeding can occur.
Expect the highest current and conductivity from the Geobacter-dominated interior; Shewanella
contributes robustness, substrate flexibility, and possible mediator or DIET support.
Long-term stability will still need monitoring — the 2019 study showed that Shewanella’s benefit
is stronger when planktonic cells remain available.

Yes — both pieces are ready.
DIET and carbon-source signals
Classic DIET (direct cell-to-cell electron transfer via conductive pili/nanowires and outer-membrane
cytochromes) is well-established between Geobacter species (first shown with G. metallireducens +
G. sulfurreducens on ethanol). Between Geobacter and Shewanella it remains a hypothesis, not a
confirmed mechanism. The strongest recent evidence is spatial: in microfluidic co-cultures,
Shewanella forms tubular colonies embedded inside a Geobacter conductive network, often without
direct electrode contact. Researchers propose that Shewanella may hand electrons to the Geobacter
matrix (possible DIET) while also cross-feeding acetate.
What is clear and repeatable is cross-feeding:
Shewanella preferentially oxidizes lactate (and broader organics) and excretes acetate.
Geobacter prefers acetate and fully oxidizes it to CO₂ while building the thick conductive biofilm.
A 5 mM acetate + 5 mM lactate mix already produced ~38 % higher current and thicker biofilms
than Geobacter alone.
Geobacter can partially oxidize lactate itself, but less efficiently and with more acetate
accumulation.
Lignocellulosic hydrolysate (or raw hemp/bamboo/wool breakdown products) is attractive for
regenerative systems but neither organism is a strong primary degrader. Mycelium or helper
cellulolytic bacteria would be needed to generate acetate/lactate-range intermediates. In that
setting the combination (cross-feeding + possible DIET) becomes more valuable than either pure
acetate or pure lactate.
Practical ranking for the living-circuit concept (highest to lowest priority right now):
1. Acetate + lactate mix (best existing co-culture data)
2. Acetate-dominant with a small lactate or hydrolysate feed (Geobacter performance +
Shewanella support)
3. Lignocellulosic hydrolysate + mycelium or helper organisms (most regenerative, needs extra
partners)
4. Lactate alone (supports Shewanella but under-utilizes Geobacter’s high-current potential)

## Message 14 — Grok

_(worked for 11s)_

Yes — biochar has been used extensively with both.
It appears in three main roles:
Electrode or anode modifier in microbial fuel cells. Algae-derived biochars with Shewanella
have raised power densities several-fold (e.g., Ulva biochar reaching ~363 mW m⁻²). Biochar-
based electrodes also enrich Geobacter and support high current.
Electron shuttle / mediator. Biochar accelerates Fe(III) reduction by both Shewanella oneidensis
and Geobacter sulfurreducens, and promotes reductive dechlorination by Geobacter. Both
conductivity and redox-active groups (quinones etc.) contribute.
Conductive carrier that stimulates DIET. Biochar enables or strengthens direct interspecies
electron transfer between Geobacter species (and between Geobacter and methanogens) even
when the biochar itself is far less conductive than granular activated carbon. Cells attach to the
particles and exchange electrons through the material. Capacitive biochars also enrich
Geobacter under anaerobic conditions.
There is additional work on mycelium-derived or mycelium-modified biochars (spent mushroom
substrate biochar, pure mycelial biochar, fungal-hyphae composites). These are mainly studied for
adsorption, soil amendment, or structural materials rather than as living electrogenic hybrids, but the
overlap is clear: fungal networks can be carbonized into high-surface-area scaffolds that later host
electroactive bacteria, or biochar can be incorporated into living mycelium composites.
Relevance to our living-circuit idea
Biochar (or mycelium-derived biochar) could serve as:
a conductive bridge between Shewanella edge zones and Geobacter interior zones,
a redox buffer / electron reservoir,
a structural and capacitive element inside a hemp–mycelium matrix.
It is already proven with these organisms; the open question is how to integrate it into a zoned, living
mycelium composite without killing the bacteria or collapsing the oxygen/redox gradients we want.

Gerald E. Brown and gadolinium do not form a single famous collaboration. Brown is a nuclear
many-body theorist; gadolinium is an element whose nuclei and electrons sit in the same physical
neighborhood he worked in. The useful overlap is real, but it is structural, not a named Brown–
Gd paper.

Gerald Edward Brown (1926–2013)
Stony Brook distinguished professor, also long at NORDITA and briefly Princeton. PhD with Gregory
Breit at Yale (1950), D.Sc. with Rudolf Peierls at Birmingham (1957). Tom W. Bonner Prize (1982),
Hans Bethe Prize (2001), Max Planck Medal. About 400 papers; more than seventy doctoral
students, including Elliott Lieb.

What he actually built:

    Atomic physics early: Lamb shift in high-Z atoms, vacuum polarization with Geoff Ravenhall,
    Rayleigh scattering.

    Nuclear many-body theory: with Tom Kuo, effective nucleon–nucleon interactions inside nuclei;
    with Mark Bolsterli, the giant dipole resonance as a collective vibration.

    Chiral nuclear physics with Mannque Rho and Dan-Olof Riska: pions and mesons as the carriers
    of the force, not just fitted potentials.

    Later, renormalization-group treatment of the nucleon–nucleon interaction, a microscopic basis
    for nuclear structure rather than a stack of phenomenological fits.

    With Hans Bethe for nearly three decades: supernova collapse, entropy in the core, compact
    binaries, stellar-mass black holes, gamma-ray bursters.

    Also the “little bag” picture of the nucleon, and early ideas that fed quark-gluon plasma work.

Honest limit: Brown is not a gadolinium specialist. His tools (effective interactions, collective modes,
chiral symmetry, relativistic mean fields that lower the nucleon effective mass) are exactly the tools
used on rare-earth nuclei, including Gd.

behaves so extremely.

Where the two actually meet
Collective nuclei. Rare-earth nuclei around Gd are the textbook deformed rotors. Brown’s giant-
dipole and effective-interaction work is the lineage behind how those spectra are calculated. A 2025
Surrey/NPL result gave direct gamma-ray evidence of a pulsing pear-shaped octupole mode in ¹⁵⁰Gd
— collective vibration of the kind Brown spent decades formalizing. That is a descendant problem,
not his experiment.

Neutron capture and the r-process. ¹⁵⁵Gd and ¹⁵⁷Gd dominate thermal capture. Half-lives of
neutron-rich Gd isotopes also help set the rare-earth abundance peak near A ≈ 160. Brown’s late
work with Bethe was about how dense nuclear matter and weak interactions behave in collapsing
stars — the astrophysical setting where those captures matter. Again, adjacent, not coauthored.

Magnetism is a different Brown. Ferromagnetic resonance papers from the 1940s–50s are William
Fuller Brown and related authors, not Gerald E. Brown. Easy to conflate. Gd’s room-temperature
ferromagnetism and magnetocaloric effect belong to condensed-matter magnetism, not to Gerry
Brown’s nuclear theory.

What is solid vs. what we should not claim
Solid: half-filled 4f shell, huge neutron cross section, room-temperature ferromagnetism, clinical
MRI contrast with documented retention risk (linear agents restricted; deposition seen even with
normal kidney function; no clear neurological syndrome proven from deposition alone).

Not solid: any claim that Gerald Brown designed a gadolinium device, a resonant Gd coil, or a
medical protocol. No such paper turned up.

For the regenerative thread, the grounded hooks are narrow. Gd is a superb neutron sink and a near-
room-temperature magnetic working fluid. It is a poor structural material and a poor thing to put in a
body without a tight chelate. Any POL or docking-station use would be as a thin absorber or a
magnetocaloric test coupon, not as a bulk resonant element.

If you want to go deeper, the clean next cuts are: Brown’s chiral effective-force picture applied to
deformed Gd, the ¹⁵⁷Gd resonance parameters versus ENDF, or the magnetocaloric numbers as a
cooling breadcrumb for a lab coil. Which of those is the thread you had in mind?

That is the right Brown. Gerald V. Brown, NASA Lewis (now Glenn), is the person who put gadolinium
to work as a room-temperature magnetic heat pump. He died at home in Lakewood, Ohio, on 21
September 2025, age 87.

Who he was
Born January 1938 in Amarillo. Music scholarship at Rice, graduated 1959, then master’s and
doctorate in physics at Case Institute of Technology. Married Nancy E. Jones in 1962. About 65 years
at NASA Lewis Research Center as a physicist and structural engineer, Apollo and shuttle structures,
then magnetic bearings, superconducting machines, and turboelectric aircraft propulsion. Retired
April 2024. Several patents on the magnetic heat pump. The Royal Society recognized his
superconducting-research career.

This is not Gerald E. Brown of Stony Brook. Different person, different field.

The gadolinium result
The landmark paper is G. V. Brown, “Magnetic heat pumping near room temperature,” Journal of
Applied Physics 47, 3673 (1976), still cited close to a thousand times. NASA patent followed: US
patent on magnetic heat pumping, filed 30 November 1976, issued 17 January 1978, inventor Gerald
V. Brown, assigned to NASA.

What he measured, on polycrystalline gadolinium:

    Curie point about 293 K, the only pure rare earth that orders ferromagnetically near room
    temperature.

    A 7 T field at the Curie point releases about 4 kJ/kg under isothermal conditions, or raises the
    temperature about 14 K under adiabatic conditions.

    Rare earths beat transition metals here because the magnetic entropy sits in a large total angular
    momentum J, and you do not have to dilute the metal to drag the Curie point down to room
    temperature.

    A regenerator is required. Lattice and electronic heat capacity would otherwise swallow the
    magnetic entropy. Regeneration lets the cycle span a useful temperature lift.

The cycle on a temperature–magnetic-entropy diagram is the magnetic Brayton/Ericsson
loop: isothermal magnetization (heat rejected), cool at constant field through the regenerator,
isothermal demagnetization (heat absorbed from the load), warm at constant field back through
the regenerator.

A follow-on with S. M. Benford, “T–S diagram for gadolinium near the Curie temperature,” Journal of
Applied Physics 52, 2110 (1981), mapped the effect from 190 to 370 K in fields of 1–7 T. Maximum ΔT
still about 14 K at 7 T, peak near 292 K.

What is solid, and what is not
Solid: the magnetocaloric effect in Gd is real, repeatable, and still the benchmark other room-
temperature alloys are measured against. Brown’s 1976 device proved a regenerative magnetic
refrigerator could run near 20 °C. Later work (Gd alloys, La–Fe–Si, Mn–Fe–P) exists because he
showed the cycle, not because the 7 T number was already practical.

Limits, stated plainly:

    7 T is a superconducting-magnet field, not a permanent-magnet field. Permanent-magnet
    prototypes later ran at 1–2 T and produced only a few kelvin per stage.
    Power density is modest. The working fluid is a solid, so you move beds or move the field, and
    cycle rate is limited by heat transfer into the regenerator.

    Gadolinium is expensive, oxidizes, and is a poor structural metal. Modern devices use alloys, not
    pure Gd bricks.
    No claim here of levitation, wireless power, or a resonant coil. This is thermodynamics of spin
    entropy.

Why it still belongs in the regenerative thread
It is one of the cleanest solid-state cooling breadcrumbs we have: no refrigerant gas, heat moved by
field and regeneration, working point at human temperature. For a POL lab or a docking-node
thermal loop, the honest use is a coupon-scale magnetocaloric stage for spot cooling of coils or
bearings, not a replacement for the main thermal path. Brown’s own later Glenn work on magnetic
bearings and turboelectric machines is the adjacent hardware family.

Next cut, if you want it: the 1976 cycle drawn against a modern 1.5 T permanent-magnet stage, or
Brown’s bearing and propulsion papers as the engineering context around the heat pump.

Iodine
The useful physics is the phase change, not the solid. Solid iodine is a poor conductor and a terrible
structure. NASA-related heat-pipe surveys treated pure iodine as a candidate working fluid in the
roughly 400–500 K band: latent heat at the boiling point about 171 kJ/kg, liquid density high,
viscosity manageable. A 304 stainless pipe with a fine stainless screen wick was run, with a large
temperature drop, and it was not life-tested. Two stated disadvantages: low thermal conductivity, so
a sintered or screen wick is mandatory, and corrosiveness. A 90 % sulfur / 10 % iodine mix was also
studied because a little iodine drops the viscosity of sulfur enough to make that fluid usable.

Iodine is also now a real electric-propulsion propellant: it stores as a dense solid, sublimes, and
ionizes. That is a different machine from a heat pipe.

Limit: free iodine attacks most metals, stains, and is an irritant vapor. Any regenerative use is a
sealed coupon — a stainless heat pipe for a coil hot-spot — not an open material in a mycelium or
hemp composite. It does not belong near a resonant coil winding.

Silver
Silver is the reference conductor. If a thermal strap or a high-frequency contact has to be as good as
metal physics allows, silver is the top of the table, ahead of copper on both heat and current.
Acoustic impedance is high (~38 MRayl) because of density, but the metal is soft and damps. It is a
poor bell and a poor structural resonator.

Limits that matter in hardware:

    It tarnishes. Ag₂S raises contact resistance and spoils optical reflectivity. A coil terminal needs a
    plating strategy or a sealed joint, not bare silver in lab air.

    It is soft and creeps. Fine for a braid or a foil interlayer, poor as a load path.

    Atomic oxygen in low Earth orbit attacks it. Not an external surface material if this ever leaves
    the bench.

    Price keeps it to straps, platings, and pastes, same logic as the gadolinium note.

For POL thermal work, silver is the spot-conduction counterpart to gadolinium’s spot-cooling: move
heat from a winding hot spot into a sink. It does not pump heat. It only conducts it.

Rhodium
Rhodium’s real job is catalysis, especially NOx reduction in autocatalysts, plus a few chemical
processes (nitric acid, acetic acid). Mechanically it is hard, corrosion-proof, and high-melting, which
is why jewelers plate it and why it shows up in thermocouples (Pt–Rh) and a few spark-plug and
crucible alloys.

It is the wrong bulk material for anything we are building. Annual supply is on the order of 30 tonnes,
price is violently cyclical, and thermal conductivity is only about a third of silver’s. A rhodium flash
plate on a contact that must not tarnish is the only hardware sentence that survives the cost test.
Even that loses to gold flash for most electrical contacts, because gold is softer to bond and far
more available.

No acoustic case. No magnetic case. No cooling case beyond “it survives the temperature.”

Tin, acoustically
This is the one that earns the acoustic question, and the answer is narrower than “tin rings.”

Tin cry is real and mechanical, not a tuned resonance. Beta-tin twins when bent. The crystals
shear, and the rod crackles. It is a demo of plasticity, useful as a teaching breadcrumb, useless as
a transducer.

Wave speeds put tin with the soft metals, not the bell metals: about 3320 m/s longitudinal and 1670
m/s shear, against roughly 5900 and 3200 m/s in steel. Acoustic impedance is about 24 MRayl. A
pure tin bar is low, dull, and heavily damped.

The acoustic alloy is bronze. High-tin bronze near 20 wt% Sn is the traditional bell and gamelan
metal. Measured longitudinal speeds in that family sit near 8800 m/s in cast instrument bronze
(composition and casting move this number), with low damping and high radiation coefficient around
20 % Sn. Push tin much past that and intensity and damping both go the wrong way. So the acoustic
note is “tin in bronze,” not “tin.”
Two other tin facts that bound the use:

    Tin pest. Below 13.2 °C, white beta-tin can transform to grey alpha-tin, which is brittle and
    expanded. The transition is slow unless seeded, but it rules pure tin out of any cold structural or
    acoustic part.

    Superconducting tin. Tc is about 3.7 K. Ultrasonic work from the 1950s found that megacycle
    velocity barely changes across the transition, while attenuation in the normal state is the
    electron-viscosity loss and drops in the superconducting state. That is a cryogenic physics
    coupon, not a room-temperature resonator.

Pewter and tin-rich solders are acoustic only by accident. They are joining metals.

How this sits next to the cooling note
Gadolinium: field-driven entropy pump, 7 T for a 14 K lift, too scarce for anything but a spot stage.
Silver: passive heat spreader, best conductor we can buy, still a strap not a structure. Iodine: latent-
heat fluid in a sealed stainless pipe, corrosive, already prototyped and not life-proven. Rhodium: do
not buy it except as a plate. Tin: acoustic only inside bronze; pure tin is the cry demo and the solder.

None of these replaces a copper winding, an aluminum heat sink, or a steel resonator. They are
edge coupons.

Silver-in-silicone is a gasket and a skin-contact material, not a conductor in the bulk-metal sense.
Iodine stays on the board for water, with a hard time limit. Bromine is the wrong halogen for drinking
water. Gold is the inert end of this row, and mercury does not “coat” it so much as dissolve into it.

Silver in silicone
Two different composites get called the same thing.

Particle-filled silicone for EMI gaskets and thermal pads is silver, silver-plated aluminum, silver-
plated copper, or silver-coated glass in a silicone rubber. A published pad test at 0.8 mm thickness
put them here: silver/aluminum about 1.25 W/m·K and 0.008 Ω·cm, silver/glass about 1.51 W/m·K and
0.010 Ω·cm, silver/copper about 1.98 W/m·K and 0.004 Ω·cm. Pressure matters more than the
datasheet conductivity, because the pad’s thermal resistance is mostly contact resistance until the
filler network is squeezed into contact. These sheets are electrically conductive. They are not
isolators. Pure-silver fill was largely abandoned once silver spiked, in favor of plated-particle fills
that keep a silver surface at lower metal mass.

The other class is medical. Silver micro- and nanoparticles in prosthetic silicone raise antibacterial
effect above 50 % in short tests, stay non-toxic at the loadings tried, barely change elongation, and
harden the elastomer as the silver fraction rises. Ion release over years is not well measured. Thin
Ag–silicone films are only weakly antibacterial and only weakly conductive until the particles touch.

For a POL or travel kit this is a cord gasket, a connector seal, or a skin-contact sleeve. It is not a heat
strap. Bulk silver is ~429 W/m·K. The filled rubber is about 1–2 W/m·K. The silicone is doing the
sealing. The silver is doing conductivity and, in the medical cut, a slow ion dose.

Limits: the conductive grades will short a winding if they bridge terminals. Silver still tarnishes at
the particle surface if sulfur reaches it. Nanoparticle grades should not be treated as a proven long-
life biocide.

Iodine for long travel
Iodine stays. Tetraglycine hydroperiodide tablets release about 8 mg of iodine per liter. Iodinated
resin filters release on the order of a few to about 10 mg/L and add a physical barrier. Bacteria and
viruses are the job it does well. Giardia is only moderate. Cryptosporidium is essentially untouched,
so a filter still has to sit in front of it for field water.

The travel limit is the thyroid, not the microbe. A military-style study at about 32 mg/day for three
months raised urinary iodide, suppressed radioiodine uptake, nudged TSH up, and enlarged the
thyroid, without clinical hypo- or hyperthyroidism in healthy adults. CDC guidance is still “not for
more than a few weeks” as the sole method. Pregnant people and anyone with thyroid disease
should not use it. Europe pulled the tablets from general sale on that basis. Taste is poor, and it
stains bottles.

On a long haul the honest kit is a filter first, iodine or chlorine dioxide as the chemical backup, and a
plan to rotate off iodine after a few weeks. Resin cartridges drift down with age. One long test was
still near 3.6 ppm after eleven months, which is inside the effective window but no longer the fresh-
cartridge number.

Bromine
Bromine is the next halogen down, and it does not earn a drinking-water seat.

At room temperature it is a dense red-brown liquid, melting point about −7 °C, boiling point about
59 °C, density near 3.1 g/cm³. Chlorine is a gas at −34 °C. Bromine’s industrial life is flame
retardants, organic intermediates, and some water biocides. In spas the common form is BCDMH,
which releases both hypobromous acid and some hypochlorous acid. Bromine holds effectiveness to
a higher pH than chlorine, and bromamines smell less harsh, which is why hot tubs use it. It is a
weaker disinfectant, heavier, and more expensive. Matching chlorine’s work takes roughly 2.25 times
the dose in ppm.
Drinking-water chemistry goes the wrong way. Bromide in source water, hit with chlorine, makes
brominated disinfection byproducts that are generally more toxic than the chlorinated ones.
Bromate, formed when bromine sees sunlight or UV, is a known carcinogen. Elemental bromine is a
severe inhalational and skin hazard. NFPA health rating 3. It is not a trail tablet and not a closed-
cabin reserve.

If the travel board needs a second halogen, chlorine dioxide beats bromine. Bromine belongs in a
flame-retardant note or a spa note, not in the water canister.

Gold
Gold is the inert reference at the end of this row. Soft, dense (19.3 g/cm³), melting point 1064 °C,
electrical resistivity about 22 nΩ·m, thermal conductivity about 318 W/m·K. Worse than silver on
both conduction numbers, better than everything on corrosion. It does not oxidize in air. That is why
contacts get a gold flash: stable surface, bondable, no sulfide film. Longitudinal sound speed is low,
about 3240 m/s. It is not an acoustic metal.

Cost and softness keep it to plating and bond wire. Same rule as rhodium and the gadolinium
coupon: edge, not structure.

What mercury actually does to gold
Mercury does not plate gold. It wets it and dissolves into it.

Clean gold has an unusually thin oxide film, so liquid mercury spreads on it instead of beading.
Surface energy drops as the mercury–gold interface replaces gold–air and mercury–water. Mercury
then diffuses inward. The first surface compound is commonly written AuHg₂. Deeper in, the phases
run through Au₂Hg toward a solid solution near Au₃Hg. Reported amalgam compositions span
roughly AuHg₂ to Au₈Hg. The mixing releases heat. Silver does this too. Most other minerals in the
ore do not, which is why the old process was selective.

The amalgam is a paste or a soft solid, not gold with a coating. To separate them, the amalgam is
heated past mercury’s boiling point, 356.7 °C. Mercury leaves as vapor. Gold, boiling point near
2836 °C, stays. That vapor is the harm. Elemental mercury inhaled damages lung, gut, and nervous
system. Historical stamp mills ran crushed ore over mercury-wetted copper plates, scraped the
amalgam, and retorted it. The chemistry has not changed. The practice is restricted because the
vapor and the tailings do not stay in the retort.

Nothing in that reaction is useful to the regenerative build. Mercury attacks aluminum and many
other structural metals as well, and it has no place in a coil, a composite, or a water kit. The only
breadcrumb is the negative one: a gold contact wiped with mercury is no longer a gold contact. It is
an amalgam, soft and contaminated, and the mercury does not stay put.

Board as it stands
Silver-in-silicone: seal and EMI gasket, optionally a skin-contact sleeve. Iodine: short-leg water
disinfectant behind a filter. Bromine: off the drinking-water board. Gold: flash plate where tarnish
cannot be allowed. Mercury: excluded.

Gold flash stays a tip-and-contact note. Bromine’s real job is the organic intermediate. Hydrogen
peroxide and the ammonium ion are two different chemistries that often get filed under the same
“nitrogen and oxygen” drawer.

Gold flash, kept narrow
A gold flash is a thin plate, typically well under a micrometre, on a contact, a probe tip, or a bond
pad. It does not carry bulk current and it does not sink heat. It stops sulfide film from forming on the
metal underneath, and it stays soft enough to make a gas-tight wipe. Silver still wins on conductivity.
Gold wins only where the surface must stay clean. Useful at a connector tip, a sensor pad, or a coil
terminal that sees lab air. Not a strap, not a structure.

Bromine as an organic intermediate
Elemental bromine is the feedstock. The intermediates are the carbon–bromine compounds made
from it. Bromine is more electronegative than carbon (about 2.9 versus 2.5), so the carbon in a C–Br
bond is electrophilic. Alkyl bromides are alkylating agents. Bond dissociation energies fall in a clean
row: C–F about 115 kcal/mol, C–Cl about 84, C–Br about 72, C–I about 58. Bromine is the practical
middle. Strong enough to survive handling, weak enough to leave when a nucleophile arrives.

What industry actually buys:

    Flame retardants are the largest use. Tetrabromobisphenol-A is the workhorse, built into circuit-
    board resins and some foams. The bromine interrupts gas-phase radical combustion. That is a
    materials fact, and also the reason many of those compounds are now restricted: they persist
    and migrate.

    Alkyl bromides are temporary handles. Hydrogen bromide adds to an alkene to give an alkyl
    bromide. Free-radical conditions flip the orientation and give 1-bromoalkanes, which go on to
    amines and quaternary ammonium salts. Ethyl bromide, n-butyl bromide, dibromomethane, and
    lauryl bromide are catalog intermediates for pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, surfactants, and
    viscosity modifiers. The bromine is often there only for one step, then displaced.
    A smaller set of drugs keep the bromine in the final molecule. The larger atom can block a
    metabolic soft spot or change tissue distribution. Fluorine and chlorine are used more often for
    that. Bromine is the special case, not the default.

    Hydrobromic acid and anhydrous HBr sit beside elemental bromine as the inorganic feeds.
    Anhydrous HBr also shows up in plasma etching.

For the regenerative board this is a joining and a materials note, not a water note. A C–Br bond is a
synthetic handle. A brominated resin is a flame-retardant coupon with a persistence cost. Neither
belongs in a coil, a water kit, or a mycelium composite. Quaternary ammonium salts made from long-
chain alkyl bromides are the one downstream product that touches the ammonium section below:
they are surfactants and biocides, not fertilizers.

Hydrogen peroxide
H₂O₂ is a carrier of active oxygen that leaves only water and oxygen if it decomposes cleanly.

That decomposition is slow in a clean, stabilized solution and fast on the wrong surface. Variable-
valence metal ions (iron, copper, manganese), dirt, and many container alloys catalyze it. Silver,
platinum, and manganese oxides are the classic heterogeneous catalysts. Stabilizers such as

Selectivity is a race between paths, not a property a bond carries by itself. Acoustics can align some
ordered liquids and can herd particles. It does not line up ordinary molecules into a chosen bond.
Two selectors, not one
A bond forms when a collision has enough energy and the right geometry. Which bond wins is
settled by one of two clocks.

Kinetic control follows the lower activation energy. The product that forms fastest dominates while
the reaction is irreversible, cold, or quenched early. Thermodynamic control follows the lower free
energy of the products. Given time, heat, and a path back, the more stable product takes over even if
it formed more slowly.

The ratio of two competing rates is set by the gap in barriers:

[MATH, reconstructed: k₁/k₂ = (A₁/A₂) · exp(−(E_a,1 − E_a,2)/(RT))]

A small gap in Ea becomes a large selectivity at low temperature, and washes out as temperature
rises. The thermodynamic ratio is a different exponential, in ΔG° of the products, not in the barriers.
Classic cases are the endo Diels–Alder product (faster, orbital overlap in the transition state) against
the exo product (more stable), and the less-substituted enolate (bulky base, cold) against the more-
substituted enolate (equilibrated).

Orientation is already inside the rate. Collision theory writes the prefactor with a steric factor. Most
encounters miss the angle the transition state requires. Enzymes and heterogeneous catalysts raise
selectivity by holding that angle still, not by changing the bond-dissociation energy of the free
molecule. The C–Br handle from the last note is this in miniature: the carbon is electrophilic because
bromine pulls charge, and the bond is weak enough to leave when a nucleophile arrives on the right
vector. Selectivity is the vector plus the barrier, not the element.

Bond formation is a crossing of a barrier, measured in turnovers or in a second-order rate constant,
not in a universal clicks-per-second for a bond type. Order of magnitude, for orientation:

    A diffusion-limited encounter in water sits near 10⁹ to 10¹⁰ M⁻¹ s⁻¹. Geometry and solvent
    already throw most collisions away.

    A covalent step with a modest barrier is slower by the Arrhenius factor. Ten kilocalories per mole
    at room temperature is a factor of about 10⁻⁷ on the rate.

    dominates. The fourth N–H bond is real, and it is also reversible the moment the proton can
    leave.

    Peroxide decomposition is the opposite lesson. The O–O bond is weak, but the uncatalyzed rate
    in a clean bottle is slow. Silver, platinum, manganese oxide, or a trace of iron collapses the
    barrier. Selectivity here is “which surface the molecule finds,” and contamination is a catalyst.
So bonding rate is local. Same atoms, different barrier, different clock. Acoustic drive does not
appear in that equation unless it changes temperature, concentration, or the path to the surface.

Acoustic alignment, where it exists
It exists for ordered fluids and for particles. It does not exist as a general molecular-bond aligner.

Liquid crystals are the clean case. Nematic molecules already want a common director. Ultrasound
vibration of the glass plates, tens of kilohertz, tilts that director through acoustic radiation force,
which follows the spatial gradient of the pressure amplitude. Work at about 44 and 71 kHz showed
the orientation pattern locking to the flexural nodes and antinodes. Larger amplitude, larger tilt. A
variable-focus liquid-crystal lens uses the same effect. Surface acoustic waves can also move
defects, with streaming flow doing as much as the pressure field. Power is low and heating is small
compared with an electric field. The molecules were already aligned. Sound re-points the director. It
does not create the order.

Particles in a standing wave are the second real case. Acoustic radiation force pushes them toward
nodes or antinodes according to density and compressibility contrast. Elongated particles also feel
an acoustic torque and lie across the pressure nodes. That is acoustophoresis. The objects are
micrometres, not bonds. Secondary forces between particles add a shorter-range dipole term,
falling off steeply with distance.

What sound does not do, on present evidence, is steer a collision complex in ordinary water or in a
metal lattice onto one bond and away from another. Sonochemistry speeds some reactions, and the
mechanism is cavitation: local hot spots, radical formation, streaming. That is a change of path and
temperature, not an alignment of molecular axes. A kilohertz wave has a wavelength of centimetres.
A bond is angstroms. The field is uniform across the molecule. There is no gradient sharp enough to
pick a bond angle.

Tin cry and bronze bells sit on the other side of this line. Those are lattice shear and resonator modes
of a solid. They are not molecular alignment.

Auroral radiation is a real, measured radio emission from the upper auroral ionosphere. It sits in
the same kilohertz-to-low-megahertz band as the acoustic-alignment work, and the resemblance

What is actually radiating

low harmonics. A nominal source near 300 kHz has elementary bandwidths of order Δf/f ∼ 10⁻³.

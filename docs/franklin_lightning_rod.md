# Honoring Benjamin Franklin: the lightning rod on a building

License CC0. Labels: **Grounded** / **Plausible-untested** / **Speculative**. Prepared with Channing.

Picture this as watching a lightning rod on a building, not a kite. That framing fits the record best, and it is the part of Franklin's work that has protected lives ever since.

- **The proposal (1749–50), Grounded.** Franklin proposed pointed iron rods, grounded into moist earth, to protect buildings.
  - His *Experiments and Observations on Electricity* (London, 1751) describes a rod "placed on the outside of a building, from the highest part continued down into the moist earth".
  - Such a rod would "receive the lightning at its upper end", and by "affording it a good conveyance into the earth, will prevent its damaging any part of the building".
  - He then published practical lightning-rod instructions in *Poor Richard's Almanack* for 1753.
- **The Philadelphia experiment, Grounded.** He also proposed an *insulated* rod on a high structure to draw electricity from a storm cloud. According to Priestley, Franklin delayed it because Philadelphia had no structure tall enough, and was apparently waiting for the Christ Church steeple (Founders Online editorial note, "The Kite Experiment, 19 October 1752").
- **Marly, 10 May 1752, Grounded.** Acting on Thomas-François Dalibard's instructions (Dalibard was Franklin's French translator), a retired dragoon at Marly-la-Ville drew sparks from a tall insulated iron rod during a storm. This showed that thunderclouds are electrified and lightning is electrical. Dalibard credited Franklin, and the experiment was soon repeated across Europe (Physics Today, "Benjamin Franklin and lightning rods").
- **The kite (probably June 1752): generally accepted, details uncertain.**
  - The account comes from Franklin's own letter of 19 Oct 1752 and from Priestley's version about 15 years later.
  - The only eyewitness, his son William, left no statement, so the date and details can't be independently confirmed (Founders Online; Penn State Center for the Book, citing Dray, *Stealing God's Thunder*).
- **The cost of getting it wrong, Grounded.** Georg Wilhelm Richmann was killed in St Petersburg on 6 Aug 1753 while measuring an insulated rod during a storm. Proper grounding is the whole point.

**RGRF link:**
- A lightning rod is a designed **leaky boundary**: it gives atmospheric charge separation (R7, Takahashi riming) a safe, low-impedance path to ground (R6).
- It is also the first engineered piece of the global electric circuit (R8, ~250 kV ionospheric potential).
- This is "nature as template" done right: observe, quantify, ground it.
